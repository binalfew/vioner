# The two-guarantees defence + scope-defence-as-honest-middle

Slide 13 introduces two transferable craft skills that round out the contribution-defence shape now established across slides 9–13. First, the **two-guarantees defence**: when defending a hand-curated component, naming what the component *will not do* (two negative guarantees) beats naming what it *does* (capability list). For the KB, the two guarantees are "validation never blocks extraction" and "enrichment never invents data" — they close the two failure modes a CS panel will worry about (gatekeeping and fabrication) before the panel asks. Second, the **scope-defence-as-honest-middle**: "active, not exhaustive" extends the deck's contribution-defence chain to five slides. Every contribution slide now carries a named-and-refused dismissive reading, and the slide 13 version has a fresh structural property — it pre-empts a *scope* attack rather than a *methodology* attack.

## Implications

- The two-guarantees defence is transferable to every component that touches the inference path. Slide 14 (platform) faces the analogous attack: "what if the FastAPI layer mangles a record?" The defensive-design answer is structural — naming what the platform will not silently do (no implicit retries that double-write; no upload size limits that drop articles without UI feedback). Slide 19 (KB operational impact) inherits the same pattern: the user-testing answer is "analysts changed their behaviour because they trusted the flags, not because the flags were always right." Trust-through-constraints is the defence shape for any component the panel might suspect.
- "Validation never blocks; enrichment never invents" carries *two* honest-middle binaries in one breath — "metadata, not veto" and "mapping, not fabrication." This is the densest binary-claim moment in the deck so far. The candidate can use this as a rehearsal benchmark: by slide 13, the dismissive-reading-and-refusal pattern is supposed to be muscle-memory, and the line should come out without conscious construction.
- The complementarity claim now has two instantiations in the deck — slide 12 (focal × class weights, axes: classes × difficulty) and slide 13 (validate × enrich, axes: correctness × canonicalisation). The candidate can point to this pattern as a *methodological signature*: when two components are combined, the candidate names two distinct axes for each pair. The panel can verify the pattern across the deck; that consistency itself is the signal that the contribution is principled.
- The "Al-Shabaab in Goma" worked example is the canonical KB demonstration. Two sentences (Sentence A → successful enrichment in Somalia; Sentence B → flagged in DRC) exercise both KB roles with the *same* canonical entry. This is the deck's most efficient worked example so far — one example, two roles, three breaths. It is the pocket-example to deploy if any panellist asks for a concrete case touching the KB. It should not be deployed in the default delivery script (the slide hits 75 s without it); it is held for interrupt-driven deepening.
- The slide-13/slide-19 reserved-numbers split is the deck's *second* number-reservation pattern. Slide 12 reserves +11 F1 for slide 18; slide 13 reserves the *operational meaning* of 2.4 % and 64.3 % for slide 19. The candidate now has two cross-slide reservation patterns. The 2.4 % flag rate is *stated* on slide 13 but *deepened* on slide 19 (what 2.4 % means for analyst behaviour — re-read trigger rate, false-flag tolerance, time saved vs analyst-only baseline). This is a softer reservation than the +11 F1 hard reservation; the number is shareable on both slides, but its operational interpretation lives only on 19.

## What this does not yet establish

- The candidate has not drilled the scope defence under interruption pressure. The hardest version of the attack is "150 sounds like a token gesture; serious actor coverage needs at least 500 — what makes you confident 150 isn't underpowered?" The canned answer (active landscape + 4.2 aliases + Recommendation 2) lands when uninterrupted; under interruption, the candidate may revert to the apologetic phrasing ("we couldn't curate more in the thesis window"). A focused drill: panellist talks over Beat 3 every time the candidate starts it; candidate has to recover and deliver the scope defence in one sentence without the "we couldn't" trap.
- The candidate has not been pushed on the *quality* of the alias table — only on its existence. The harder follow-up is "how do you measure that 4.2 aliases per group is *enough*? Is there an evaluation of alias coverage on held-out documents?" The honest answer (no held-out alias-coverage benchmark; 4.2 is the descriptive mean, not a tuned hyperparameter; an alias-recall study is named in §7.4 future work) is in the thesis but isn't drilled. This is the next layer to build for a measurement-savvy panel.
- The two-axes framing for validate × enrich (correctness × canonicalisation) is *structurally* parallel to slide 12 (classes × difficulty) but the candidate has not been live-tested on stating both axis pairs back-to-back without conflating them. The muscle-memory risk is mixing the axes mid-defence — e.g., starting the slide-13 defence with "across classes" by reflex from slide 12. A back-to-back drill (recite slide-12 axes, then slide-13 axes, ten times, no errors) would close this risk before the live defence.
- The "what about brand-new armed groups not in the KB?" attack has a clean answer (raw form persists; no fabrication; analyst flags for curation), but it has not been combined with the *temporal* attack — "the armed-group landscape changed during the time your KB was curated; how do you know the KB you tested with is still valid?" The honest answer (KB was curated against ACLED snapshots from 2024; ongoing curation is the quarterly cadence proposed in §7.4; the deck's evaluation timeline is documented in §6.2) is in the qa_kit but not pre-loaded for slide 13. This is the methodologist-style follow-up to expect.
- The "why does the KB run server-side and not client-side?" attack — a more engineering-oriented follow-up — has no current canned answer. The honest defence (server-side because the KB is curated content with version control; client-side would require shipping all 150 aliases × all 200 cities × validation rules on every page load; in-memory at startup is the right deployment shape) is straightforward but isn't drilled. Low-probability attack, but worth a one-line reserved answer.

## Note on the slide-13 → slide-19 reserved-numbers split

The 2.4 % flag rate and the 64.3 % enrichment rate appear on both slides, but they do *different work* on each slide. On slide 13 they prove the KB is operationally non-trivial (the rates are large enough to matter, the rates are small enough to be defensible). On slide 19 they pay off as user-testing evidence (the rates *change analyst behaviour* — re-read triggered by flags, aggregation correctness restored by enrichment). The candidate must not collapse the slide-13 framing into the slide-19 framing during the slide-13 delivery; that flattens slide 19's punchline. The discipline: on slide 13, the rates are *engineering metrics*; on slide 19, they become *operational impact*. Two different stories, one set of numbers. This is the second instance of the cross-slide reserved-numbers technique introduced on slide 9 / deepened on slide 12 (+11 F1 reserved for slide 18).
