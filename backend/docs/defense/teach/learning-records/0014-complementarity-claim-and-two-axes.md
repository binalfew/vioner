# The complementarity claim + the two-axes mental model

Slide 12 introduces a craft skill that defends every "why use both X and Y?" question in the deck: the *complementarity claim*. When two techniques are deployed together, the defence is not "they both help" (which reads as redundancy) but "they attack *different axes* of the same problem" (which reads as principled construction). For focal × class weights, the two axes are *across classes* (rare vs common) and *across difficulty* (hard vs easy within a class). The hero line collapses to a binary contrast: "different parts of the imbalance — class weights across classes, focal across difficulty. The combination beats either alone."

## Implications

- The complementarity claim generalises across the deck. Slide 13 (KB validate AND enrich) faces the same shape — "why both validate and enrich?" — and the same defence applies: different axes (correctness vs canonicalisation), not redundancy. Slide 14 (the platform) faces "why FastAPI AND PostgreSQL AND React when a notebook would do?" — different axes (inference, persistence, presentation). Slide 22 (the five contributions) faces the meta-version of this attack: "are these five really distinct contributions?" — answer with five distinct axes, not five overlapping ones.
- The two-axes mental picture is now the canonical visual move for any "X × Y" combination in the deck. The candidate can sketch it on a whiteboard mid-defence if asked: one axis labelled, the other axis labelled, the combination as the diagonal lift. This is a transferable rehearsable artefact.
- The +11 F1 number is being *deliberately reserved for slide 18* — the candidate now has a three-step withholding pattern: (1) plant the claim on slide 12 ("the ablation shows..."); (2) name slide 18 as the location ("on slide 18..."); (3) deploy the number on slide 18 itself. This deepens the reserved-numbers technique from slide 9 — the same number can be cross-referenced across three slides without losing its punchline-position on slide 18.
- "Complementary, not redundant" is the candidate's fourth honest-middle binary, after measurement/preference (slide 9), decomposed/narrower (slide 10), synthesis/relabelling (slide 11). Four contribution slides, four binary contrasts that name and refuse a dismissive reading. The pattern is now firmly established as the deck's contribution-defence shape.
- The numerical worked examples — 130× for the across-classes axis, p=0.9 → 0.01 for the across-difficulty axis — anchor the abstract claims. NOTES.md says the user learns through worked numerical examples; both axes now have one. Future "X × Y" defences should always carry a number for each side, never just structural language.

## What this does not yet establish

- The candidate has not been live-drilled on Beat 4 (complementarity) without using the phrase "both help with imbalance." That phrase is the muscle-memory trap; the lesson includes the harder rep but a focused drill — say the complementarity beat five times in a row without any "both help" formulation — is the next muscle-memory layer to build.
- The candidate has not practised holding the +11 F1 number back when directly asked here on slide 12. The right move is to *name the location* (slide 18 / §6.6) without deploying the number; the cleanest deflection is "I'll have the full ablation table on slide 18 — the headline is plus eleven F1 on VICTIM versus plain cross-entropy." A focused drill: panellist asks "how much does it actually help?" — candidate replies in 5 seconds without quoting the number stand-alone.
- The γ = 2 defence has the literature reference (Lin et al. 2017) and the failure modes (γ=1 smaller gain, γ=3 instability) but the candidate hasn't been asked the harder follow-up: "did you do a proper grid search on γ?" The honest answer (no — three values tested, sufficient for an applied thesis; full grid search would require more compute than available) is in the qa_kit but isn't drilled.
- The cap-at-10 answer ("empirical, unclipped VICTIM ≈ 22 destabilises") is reserved but the candidate may be probed on *why those specific destabilisation signals matter* — gradient norm exploding, loss going NaN, validation F1 collapsing in the first epoch. Naming a specific symptom under interruption is harder than the canned answer makes it look.
- The "did you try dice loss?" attack has a clean concession + future-work answer, but the deeper attack — "you say focal loss has more direct prior work, but Sudre et al. 2017 proposed dice loss specifically for imbalance" — is unaddressed. The honest defence (dice loss originates in segmentation, not token classification; the transfer to BIO has fewer established baselines) is the next layer to build for a methodology-savvy panel.
