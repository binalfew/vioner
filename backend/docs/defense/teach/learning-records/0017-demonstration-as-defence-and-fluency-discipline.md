# Demonstration-as-defence + fluency discipline

Slide 15 is the deck's first **demonstration** slide and the candidate's first encounter with a defence shape that is not argumentative. Every slide from 9–14 was a contribution defence — name a dismissive reading and refuse it (slides 9–13), or own the dismissive reading and elevate above it (slide 14). Slide 15 cannot be defended that way because it makes no contestable claim — it shows the system running. The defence shape is **fluency**: walking the panel through a 10-step pipeline and a 6-entity worked example so confidently that the integration looks effortless. Where slide 14's failure mode was rhetorical (ceding the framing), slide 15's failure mode is **muscle-memory**: stumbling on the pipeline list, mis-naming an entity, mis-stating a latency number. The defence is therefore drill-heavy, not argument-heavy.

## Implications

- The 10-step pipeline list is a memorisation artefact, not an argument structure. The candidate's drill protocol — recite the list five times eyes-closed, restart on any stumble — is the slide's central preparation activity. The predicted stumble points are steps 4–6 (BIO decode → confidence filter → 5W1H group, where the ordering is non-obvious because confidence filter sits between BIO decode and 5W1H group, which a candidate who learned the lifecycle from concepts_explained.md §4.4–4.5 may have ordered as confidence filter → BIO decode). The slide's verbatim ordering is the source of truth; the candidate must drill the slide's order specifically, not the concepts-doc's order. The 4-cluster mnemonic (intake / model / post-proc / augment / deliver) is the working-memory scaffold that holds the list together when fluency wavers.
- The three normalisation flourishes — *Tuesday → calendar date*, *Mogadishu → Somalia*, *twelve + "at least" qualifier* — are the slide's highest-bandwidth moments. They are the difference between "the model extracts text" (which is unremarkable) and "the system produces structured records analysts can query against" (which is the operational value the deck is selling). Pacing them deliberately during delivery is the load-bearing discipline; rushing them collapses the slide back into a generic NER demo. The discipline transfers to every demonstration moment elsewhere in the deck — the candidate's habit should now be to identify the *normalisation moments* in any demo and pace them visibly.
- The latency Pareto (75 / 15 / 10) is structurally a continuation of slide 14's in-process-architecture claim. The "everything else is 10 % because the KB is in-memory" line is the explicit verbal bridge between slides 14 and 15. The candidate now has two cross-slide bridges: (a) the +11 F1 reservation that links slides 12 and 18, and (b) the in-memory-KB causal chain that links slides 14 and 15. Slide-to-slide bridges are how the deck reads as a coherent argument rather than a sequence of independent claims; the candidate should be alert to where else explicit bridges can be planted (slide 13's two guarantees → slide 15's KB-failure attack response is another such bridge that has been used in the lesson).
- The close — "that's what 'integration evaluated end-to-end' looks like in one article — schema, model, KB, taxonomy, platform, all touching one record" — is the deck's first verbatim self-citation. The candidate is now repeating a slide-14 hero phrase verbatim on slide 15, and naming the five-component structure (schema / model / KB / taxonomy / platform) that slide 22 will close on. This is the deck's longest-range structural callback so far: a phrase planted on slide 14, re-deployed on slide 15, and resolved on slide 22 forms a three-slide arc the panel can audit. The candidate's discipline must include treating these self-citations as verbatim — paraphrasing them breaks the audit trail.
- The slide 15 lesson does NOT deploy per-entity F1 numbers (those are slide 17), does NOT deploy the +11 F1 ablation (slide 18), does NOT deploy the UAT Likert details (slide 19). The slide-15 worked example shows six categories doing the work; the quantitative evidence that they do it well is sequenced across slides 17 / 18 / 19. The reservation discipline is now multi-slide: slide 15 reserves to slide 17, which reserves to slide 18, which reserves to slide 19. The candidate must hold this whole reservation graph in working memory during the live defence — using a reserved number on the wrong slide collapses the punchline-position of the slide it belongs to. The drill discipline: every slide-15 rehearsal should explicitly identify the numbers that *could* be deployed but *should not* be.

## What this does not yet establish

- The candidate has not been drilled on the 10-step list with an interrupt mid-recital. The most damaging form of the interrupt is the panellist who asks a clarifying question between step 5 and step 6, then expects the candidate to resume from step 7. The discipline for recovery is to silently restart from the top of the cluster the interrupt landed in — if the interrupt lands at step 5 (confidence filter), restart from step 4 (BIO decode); if at step 8 (taxonomy), restart from step 7 (KB). Restarting from step 1 reads as "doesn't remember"; resuming from the wrong step reads as "lost the place". Cluster-anchored restart is the right strategy and has not been drilled.
- The canonical worked example has been delivered with a single sentence. A multi-actor sentence ("Al-Shabaab and Boko Haram operatives clashed with Nigerian and Cameroonian forces near Lake Chad") has not been rehearsed. The likely panel attack is "what happens when there are multiple actors of the same type?" — the honest answer (multiple PERPETRATOR spans get separate KB lookups; if the analyst's downstream consumer expects one canonical actor, the architectural commitment is to surface both and let the consumer aggregate) is in the design but isn't in the rehearsal set. This is the slide-15 follow-up to expect on the harder end of the question distribution.
- The "what if confidence filter drops a true positive?" attack has no canned answer. The honest defence (per-category thresholds were tuned on validation set to minimise expected operational cost — false-positive casualty counts hurt more than false-negative dates; the threshold-search is documented in §5.5) is reasonable but the candidate may not have rehearsed naming "operational cost asymmetry" as the threshold-tuning principle. The phrase is from the concepts_explained.md §4.4 framing; it has not yet appeared in any prior lesson and may need its own micro-lesson if the panel probes confidence filtering.
- The candidate has not rehearsed the cross-slide attack "your worked example shows what the system does on one article; how do we know it generalises?" The right answer (slides 16, 17, 18 contain the evidence on the 10 000-document validation set; the worked example is a single illustration, not a generalisation claim) is straightforward but the candidate may revert to defending the worked example itself, which is a category error. The correct posture is to concede the worked example is illustrative and immediately pivot to the next three slides as the evidence. This pivot has not been drilled.
- The deck's reservation graph (slide 15 → 17 → 18 → 19 for quantitative evidence) is now load-bearing across four slides. The candidate has not yet been tested on whether they can hold all four reservations live. A focused drill — recite slide 15 in full, then immediately have a panellist ask "what's the per-entity F1?", "what's the ablation gain?", and "what's the UAT score?" in sequence — would surface whether the candidate correctly routes each number to its home slide rather than depositing them all on slide 15. This is the next layer of reservation discipline to build.

## Note on the structural shift from slide 14 to slide 15

Slides 9–14 occupy the deck's "argue the contribution" phase. Slide 15 begins the deck's "show the contribution" phase, which continues through slides 16–19 (headline F1, per-entity F1, ablation, KB operational impact + UAT). The defence shape across slides 15–19 is fundamentally **evidence-based** rather than argument-based — the candidate is asking the panel to look at numbers, walkthroughs, and user-testing outcomes rather than rhetorical structures. The slide-15 lesson is therefore the first lesson in the *evidence section* of the deck, and the fluency discipline it teaches (drill the list, pace the flourishes, name the cross-slide bridges) is the discipline that should generalise to the next four lessons. The candidate's preparation rhythm should shift accordingly — from "rehearse the binary contrast" (slides 9–13) and "rehearse the agree-and-elevate" (slide 14) to "rehearse the list and the numbers" (slides 15–19). This is a real shift in the rehearsal mode, and it is worth naming explicitly so the candidate can adjust their rehearsal time allocation.
