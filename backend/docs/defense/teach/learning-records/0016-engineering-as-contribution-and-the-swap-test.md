# Engineering-as-contribution defence + the swap test

Slide 14 introduces a defence shape that is structurally different from every prior contribution slide and that the candidate has not rehearsed before. Every slide from 9–13 used the **honest-middle binary** pattern (name the dismissive reading, refuse it with a precise positive claim). Slide 14 cannot do that — at the surface level, the dismissive reading ("this is engineering, not research") is *correct* about the individual choices. The slide-14 move is **agree-and-elevate**: own the engineering framing at the parts level, then name a level above the parts (the integration evaluated end-to-end) where the research contribution lives. The **swap test** — "swapping React for Vue would change implementation, not contribution" — is the concrete tool that performs the elevation in one sentence. Together, the agree-and-elevate move and the swap test are the deck's only two-level contribution defence.

## Implications

- The swap test is now part of the candidate's defence toolkit and generalises to any "why X specifically?" attack on slide 14. The discipline is to never defend the framework on its merits — concede the swap, name the engineering reason for the chosen one in one phrase, pivot to integration. The deeper transferable skill is recognising the *trap shape*: when a question pulls the candidate toward arguing about *technology*, the right answer is to argue about *methodology* instead. The swap test is what performs that pivot.
- The agree-and-elevate move is a new addition to the deck's contribution-defence vocabulary. The candidate previously had three shapes: (1) honest-middle binary (slides 9–11); (2) complementarity claim with two distinct axes (slides 12–13); (3) two-guarantees defence via negative constraints (slide 13's hero line). Slide 14 adds a fourth: (4) agree-and-elevate, where the dismissive reading is correct at one level but the contribution lives at a higher level. This is the candidate's most rhetorically sophisticated move; it requires owning the attack before redirecting it, and it carries the risk that owning the attack will be read as concession if the elevation step is not delivered with full confidence. The candidate should not deploy this move on any slide where they are uncertain about the elevation step.
- The slide-6 callback is critical and load-bearing. The integration claim ("no academic work has integrated schema + model + KB + UI in this domain") is unprovable in isolation — it requires the audit reference to the related-work landscape from slide 6 to be checkable. If the candidate skips the callback during slide 14 delivery, the integration claim collapses to an assertion. The drill discipline: every slide-14 rehearsal must include "the empty cell on the related-work landscape from slide 6" as a verbatim phrase, even when delivery is being compressed.
- The +11 F1 / slide 18 reservation pattern (slide 12), the operational-impact / slide 19 reservation pattern (slide 13), and now the UAT-Likert / slide 19 reservation pattern (slide 14) form a triple. Slide 19 is now the deck's most numbers-loaded slide — it inherits operational rates from slide 13 (2.4 %, 64.3 %) and UAT Likert details from slide 14 (4.6 / 5, 4.0 / 5, six tasks, five participants). The slide 19 lesson, when it comes, will need to manage these reservations explicitly — the user-testing section should *deepen* slide 14's UAT mention without re-stating the n=5 / 6/6 framing that slide 14 already deployed.
- "Integration" is now the load-bearing word on slide 14, joining "complementarity" (slide 12) and "two roles, one resource" (slide 13). The candidate's contribution-vocabulary across the four research-claim slides (12–14, plus 22) is: *complementarity, two roles, integration, contribution-set*. These are the four structural claims that justify the system being more than the sum of its parts. Slide 22's five-contribution close depends on slide 14 having already established "integration" as a contribution-worthy noun.

## What this does not yet establish

- The candidate has not been live-tested on the agree-and-elevate move under interruption. The hardest interrupt is when a panellist talks over Beat 2 with "but anyone can build a FastAPI service" — the natural response is to defend the engineering, which loses the framing. The disciplined response is to concede ("yes — and that's the point; the parts are engineering, the integration is the research") and pivot. A focused drill: panellist interrupts every time the candidate says "FastAPI" or "React" with "anyone can do that", and the candidate has to recover the integration claim in two sentences without defending the framework.
- The candidate has not drilled the swap test against a hostile follow-up: *"if the swap doesn't matter, why did you spend chapter 5 documenting it?"* The honest answer (chapter 5 documents implementation choices for reproducibility, not for novelty claims; the contribution chapter is §7, not §5) is a structural distinction between *reproducibility* documentation and *contribution* claim that the candidate may not yet have separated cleanly. This is the harder follow-up to expect from a methodology-savvy panel.
- The car-vs-engine analogy is in the candidate's pocket but has not been tested for the failure mode where a panellist takes the analogy literally and asks "what's the steering wheel? what are the brakes?" The right response is to graciously concede the analogy is a vehicle for the framing ("the analogy is shorthand for components-as-deliverable; the four layers are the concrete components") rather than extend the metaphor. Over-extending an analogy in a defence is a known failure mode — the analogy must be deployable and abandonable in the same breath.
- The "30 k articles/year" monolith justification has been planted but the harder follow-up — "what happens at 300 k?" — is not pre-loaded. The honest answer (horizontal scaling via load balancer + multiple FastAPI workers; the in-process KB is replicable per worker; the architecture supports this without changes) is straightforward but the candidate may not have rehearsed it. Low-probability attack from an AAU CS panel, but worth a one-line reserved answer.
- The UAT defence is structured around "n=5, six tasks, all completed" without yet rehearsing the *statistical* attack — "n=5 isn't statistically significant; how do you defend the sample size?" The honest answer (UAT is not inferential statistics; it is qualitative validation of operability; the five-participant sample was selected for *role coverage* — analysts, academic, developers — not for statistical power; full statistical UAT would require a different study design and budget) is in the deck's defensive frame but not muscle-memorised. This is the slide-19 attack to pre-load before the live defence.

## Note on the two-level binary

The slide-14 binary — "engineering pragmatism at the parts level · research integration at the system level" — is the only two-level binary in the deck. Every other contribution slide carries a binary that resolves at a single level (decomposed-not-narrower, complementary-not-redundant, etc.). The two-level structure makes slide 14 the highest-risk delivery in the contribution arc: if the panel only registers the first half (engineering), they update toward "this is not research"; if the panel registers both halves, they update toward "research at a level above the parts I see." The candidate's delivery discipline on slide 14 must therefore weight the second half — *integration evaluated end-to-end* — with deliberate emphasis. Rushing it, or letting it fade into a clause, is the single largest delivery risk on slide 14 and probably on the entire contribution arc.
