Lesson 09 · Slide 7 — The four operational gaps

State four operational gaps in 90 seconds — and defeat "why four and not three?"

Slide 7 is the heart of the problem statement. It's where the abstract "empty cell" from slide 6 becomes four concrete things the analyst can't do. Every contribution in slides 10-14 closes exactly one of these.

Mission link. Commit to the four-part contribution shape. Skill type. Operational framing + "independently necessary" defence. Time. 15 minutes.

Lesson 8 positioned VioNER in the empty cell of the competitive landscape. Slide 7 does the next move: what does the empty cell actually mean operationally?. The answer is four specific things the analyst can't do today. Each one will be closed by one contribution. If the panel accepts the four-gap structure, every contribution slide that follows feels inevitable rather than arbitrary.

The delivery risk on this slide. Slide 7 is structurally similar to slide 5 (four-item enumeration), so it's tempting to deliver it with the same cadence. Don't. Slide 5 listed technical challenges; slide 7 lists operational deficits. The grammar of each item is different: "What the analyst CAN'T get" not "Why the model can't do this." Hit the "CAN'T" with vocal weight — that's the signal you're speaking the analyst's language.

2 · The new craft skill — operational framing

Every gap on this slide is stated in user terms, not technical terms. That's deliberate. The panel has heard you talk about loss functions and BERT and class imbalance; on slide 7, you switch to the analyst's vocabulary. This signals: "I understand who this is for, not just how it works."

Technical framing (wrong)

"Plain CE doesn't handle class imbalance well."

"Generic NER lacks domain KB integration."

"There's no end-to-end deployable artefact in the literature."

Operational framing (right)

"Analysts don't see victims, action verbs, or casualties — the operationally critical entities."

"Output isn't sanity-checked against world knowledge."

"No system an analyst can deploy and run without ML expertise."

The rule of operational framing

If the sentence describes what the technology is missing → it's technical framing. If the sentence describes what the user can't do → it's operational framing. Slide 7 is all operational. Every later contribution slide will be partly technical; but slide 7 is the moment where you speak the analyst's language so the panel knows you can.

3 · The 90-second script, in six beats

0
Opener ~10 seconds
"The four operational gaps — the heart of the contribution. I state them operationally, in terms of what the analyst CAN'T get today."
"CAN'T" is the orientation cue. The capital letters on the slide signal it; your vocal weight on "CAN'T" makes the panel hear it. Pause briefly after.
1
Gap 1 — fast structured 5W1H ~15 seconds
"Gap one: fast, structured, role-distinguished 5W1H output. ACLED has it slowly; automated tools give flat or wrong-shape output."
Three adjectives — fast, structured, role-distinguished — say them as a unit. The "ACLED... automated tools..." contrast tells the panel where today's solutions fall short.
2
Gap 2 — rare-entity recovery ~15 seconds
"Gap two: automated tools recover dates and locations but miss victims, casualties, action verbs."
"miss victims, casualties, action verbs" is the operational version of "rare-entity recovery". Land "miss" with weight — that's what makes it operational.
3
Gap 3 — trust + canonicalisation ~15 seconds
"Gap three: output isn't sanity-checked against world knowledge or canonicalised across surface forms."
Two sub-deficits: world-knowledge validation, and surface-form canonicalisation. The "or" joins them — say it like one combined deficit, not two separate items.
4
Gap 4 — analyst-deployable ~15 seconds
"Gap four: no system an analyst can actually deploy and run without ML expertise."
"Without ML expertise" is the operational constraint. The analyst isn't a developer — they need something that just runs.
5
The defence — "why four and not three?" ~20 seconds
"If asked 'why four and not three?' — no existing system fills all four, and each is independently necessary for the analyst's job. A perfectly structured output that arrives too late is useless; a fast output that misses victims is useless."
This is the most important beat. The two concrete examples (slow-structured / fast-incomplete) are what make "independently necessary" feel argued, not asserted. Land both examples slowly. End on the second "useless" with a clean stop. Advance to slide 8.

4 · The "independently necessary" matrix

The defence beat hinges on showing that any THREE gaps met = analyst still can't do their job. Memorise this table — it lets you defend "why four and not three" from any angle a panellist comes from.

What's metWhat's missingWhat goes wrong
Structured 5W1H + rare entities + validationNot deployableBeautiful output the analyst can never run. Stays on a researcher's laptop.
Rare entities + validation + deployableNot structured 5W1HFast output in the wrong shape. Analyst has to restructure every record.
Structured + validation + deployableNo rare-entity recoveryBeautiful empty cells where victim, casualties, action should be.
Structured + rare entities + deployableNo validationPlausible-looking but factually unchecked output. Geopolitically risky.
The argument in one line. Each row of the table is an existence proof that three gaps closed ≠ analyst-usable. Therefore all four are necessary; therefore the contribution is a four-part integration, not a single component.

6 · Three pre-loaded interrupts

"Why four and not three? Couldn't gaps 1 and 4 collapse?"
"Gap one is about the output shape — fast, structured, role-distinguished. Gap four is about the system shape — an analyst can run it. ACLED has gap one met (the output shape is structured) but gap four unmet (no analyst can run it; it requires the ACLED coding team). Generic deployable NER systems have gap four met (anyone can run them) but gap one unmet (output is flat, no role distinction). The two gaps fail independently — they're not the same thing."
Concrete examples on both sides. Don't define the gaps abstractly — show them with ACLED and generic NER as anchor points.
"Aren't gaps 2 and 3 both about quality?"
"Gap two is about recall — finding the rare entities at all. Gap three is about validation — checking that what's found is geographically and factually plausible. Recall without validation produces hallucinated victims; validation without recall validates nothing. They address different failure modes."
"Different failure modes" is the disarming phrase. Recall failure and validation failure aren't the same kind of error.
"Is this just a checklist? Where does the real contribution sit?"
"The contribution is the integration. Each gap maps to one component — schema, model, KB, platform. Each component on its own has precedent in the literature. What's new is closing all four simultaneously, evaluated end-to-end. The integration is the contribution; the four-gap structure is how I justify why integration is necessary."
This is the integration thread from slide 2, made operational. Use the exact phrase "the integration is the contribution" — it's the through-line.

7 · Timed practice

Six beats, ~90 seconds. Range: 80-105 seconds with the defence beat (#5) taking proper time.

0.0 s
The harder rep. Cover the slide. Recite all four gap topics in order — fast structured 5W1H · rare-entity recovery · trust + canonicalisation · analyst-deployable. Then deliver the "perfectly structured / fast output" defence example. If you swap gap 2 and gap 3 order, or forget the two concrete examples in the defence, do another pass. The order matters because it tracks the analyst's workflow: structure first (what shape is the output?), then content (does it have the right things?), then trust (can I rely on it?), then access (can I run it?).

8 · Quiz

Q1. Which framing belongs on slide 7?

Pick the operationally framed statement.

  • "Plain cross-entropy fails to learn rare entity classes under severe imbalance."
  • "Automated tools recover dates and locations but miss victims, casualties, action verbs."
  • "BERT NER models lack domain-specific KB lookups."
  • "Current NER systems use generic schemas not optimised for African conflict events."

Q2. A panellist asks: "Couldn't gaps 1 and 4 collapse into one?"

Pick the strongest one-sentence rebuttal.

  • "They're conceptually different — output shape vs system shape."
  • "The thesis defines them separately, so they should stay separate."
  • "ACLED has gap one met (structured output) but gap four unmet (no analyst can run it); generic deployable NER has gap four met but gap one unmet. They fail independently."
  • "Without gap four, the structured output would be useless."

Q3. What is the strategic purpose of the defence beat ("If asked why four and not three...")?

Pick the right reading.

  • To acknowledge that the four-gap structure could be controversial.
  • To pad the script to hit the 90-second budget.
  • To remind the panel that there are exactly four contributions.
  • To pre-empt the most likely panel attack on the contribution structure. By stating the defence proactively, the candidate prevents a hostile interrupt that would otherwise derail the narrative.

9 · Fluency checklist

  • I hit "CAN'T" with vocal weight on the opener — signals the slide is in the analyst's vocabulary.
  • I deliver all four gaps in order (structured → rare entities → trust → deployable) without confusion.
  • I say "miss victims, casualties, action verbs" in operational tone, not technical tone.
  • I deliver the defence beat with both concrete examples — slow-structured AND fast-incomplete.
  • I can answer "couldn't gaps 1 and 4 collapse?" by citing ACLED and generic NER as failure-mode anchors.
  • I can answer "aren't gaps 2 and 3 both about quality?" by distinguishing recall from validation.
  • My last timed rep was between 80 and 105 seconds.
If all seven are ticked, slide 7 commits the panel to the four-part contribution shape. Every later slide (10-14) is now a 1:1 response to a gap they've already accepted is real and operationally necessary. The "integration is the contribution" thread (lesson 3) is now demonstrated, not just asserted.
