Lesson 01 · Focal loss

Defend focal loss in 90 seconds

From "I understand focal loss" to "I can deliver it under pressure" — the bridge a defense needs.

Mission link. Defend modelling decisions without stumbling. Difficulty. Delivery, not theory. Time. 12–15 minutes.

You already understand the mechanics: focal loss multiplies cross-entropy by $(1 - p_y)^\gamma$ to suppress easy-correct tokens. You can do the row-by-row arithmetic in the worked example in concepts_explained.md §D1. Mission accomplished there.

The gap now is fluent oral delivery. A panel question is not a homework problem — you have ten seconds before silence becomes uncomfortable. This lesson compresses everything you know about focal loss into one rehearsable 30-second pitch, three pre-loaded follow-up answers, and one whiteboard sketch you can produce from memory.

That is the whole lesson. By the end you should be able to recite the pitch without notes and answer any of the three follow-ups in one breath.

1 · The 30-second pitch (memorise this verbatim)

This is what you say if a panellist asks "What is focal loss and why did you use it?". Three sentences. About 30 seconds at calm delivery speed.

"Focal loss is cross-entropy multiplied by a $(1 - p_y)^2$ focusing factor that suppresses tokens the model already gets right, so the optimiser concentrates on the hard ones. We combined it with inverse-frequency class weights so the rare classes — VICTIM, ACTION, CASUALTIES — contribute meaningfully to the gradient rather than being drowned out by the 78% O tokens. The ablation in section 6.6 shows the combination lifted VICTIM F1 by 11 points over plain cross-entropy without hurting any other entity. That is the empirical defence for the loss-function contribution."
Why these three sentences and not others?
  • Sentence 1 names what focal loss is in one mathematical idea (the focusing factor).
  • Sentence 2 explains why we pair it with class weights — anticipating the inevitable "why not just one or the other?" follow-up.
  • Sentence 3 closes with the quantified empirical claim (+11 F1) — the headline that makes the contribution defensible.
The pitch is structured so that even if the panel cuts you off after the second sentence, you have already delivered the key technical content.

Dry-run protocol

  1. Read the pitch aloud three times, slowly.
  2. Cover it and recite from memory. Don't paraphrase — match the wording.
  3. Time yourself. If you finish in less than 25 seconds you're rushing; over 40 seconds you're padding.
  4. Now imagine a panellist asks "What is focal loss?" mid-sip of coffee. Deliver the pitch from memory.

2 · The three predicted follow-ups

If your pitch is good, the panel will pursue exactly one of three follow-ups. Pre-load the answer to each — one or two sentences, no improvisation.

Attack 1. "Why $\gamma = 2$ and not $\gamma = 1$ or $\gamma = 3$?"
"$\gamma = 2$ is the literature default from Lin et al. 2017 — the original focal loss paper. We tested $\gamma = 1$, which gave smaller gains because the focusing factor was too gentle, and $\gamma = 3$, which destabilised training because the suppression of easy tokens was too aggressive. $\gamma = 2$ is the empirically stable choice."
Land the citation (Lin et al. 2017). It moves the question from "did you arbitrarily pick this?" to "you followed the established literature."
Attack 2. "Why cap class weights at 10?"
"Without the cap, the rarest class weight comes out around 22 — VICTIM was the binding case. That made early-epoch gradients too large, the optimiser overshot, and training destabilised. Empirically, 10 keeps rare classes elevated enough to learn but small enough that training remains stable."
"Empirically" is the magic word — it concedes the choice was tuned, not derived, which is honest and disarms the "is this arbitrary?" line of attack.
Attack 3. "Why focal loss and class weights — wouldn't one suffice?"
"They attack different aspects of the same imbalance. Class weights rebalance across classes — VICTIM tokens get a per-token boost. Focal loss rebalances within a class — easy tokens get suppressed regardless of which class they're in. The ablation in section 6.6 shows the combination beats either alone — focal alone gave +8 F1 on VICTIM, weights alone gave +7, but combined we got +11."
The "across vs within" framing is the conceptual key. Memorise that exact phrase.

3 · The whiteboard sketch

If a panellist hands you a marker and asks "show me the focusing factor", this is what you draw. One axis, one curve, one annotation.

Talking points while you draw it

  1. Draw the axes first. Label x: $p_y$ — model's probability on the true label. Label y: focal factor.
  2. Draw the curve from top-left to bottom-right, dipping sharply in the middle.
  3. Annotate three points: p = 0.2 → factor ≈ 0.64 (hard, contributes most of CE), p = 0.5 → 0.25 (medium), p = 0.9+ → ≈ 0.01 (easy, suppressed).
  4. Say: "This is the dimmer switch between the model's confidence and how much that token contributes to learning. Plain cross-entropy is a constant factor of 1 — every token contributes the same. Focal loss dims the easy ones."

4 · Quiz — match attack to answer

Tap each option to test yourself. The page tells you immediately whether you're right.

Q1. A panellist asks: "Why pair focal loss with class weights instead of using just one of them?"

Pick the best one-sentence answer.

  • "Because the ablation in section 6.6 says so."
  • "Class weights rebalance across classes; focal loss rebalances within a class. They attack different aspects of the imbalance, so the combination beats either alone — focal alone gave +8 F1 on VICTIM, weights alone +7, combined +11."
  • "Focal loss alone wasn't enough on rare classes, so we added class weights as a safety net."
  • "Class weights handle the rare entities; focal loss handles all the rest."

Q2. "Why $\gamma = 2$?"

Pick the most defensible one-sentence answer.

  • "It was the value that gave the best result in my experiments."
  • "Higher gammas would have helped more but training was unstable."
  • "It's the literature default from Lin et al. 2017. We tested $\gamma = 1$ and $\gamma = 3$ — $\gamma = 1$ gave smaller gains, $\gamma = 3$ destabilised training."
  • "$\gamma$ is the focusing parameter — we just used the value that's standard in object detection."

Q3. "Class weights and focal loss — is one of them redundant?"

Which phrase belongs in your one-sentence answer?

  • "One handles common classes, the other handles rare ones."
  • "Across-class rebalancing versus within-class hard-mining."
  • "Focal loss is more important — class weights are a small boost on top."
  • "They are slightly redundant but I kept both for safety."
