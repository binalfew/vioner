1 · Where this slide sits in the arc

Slide 14 made the claim: "the integration is the contribution." Slide 15 is the demonstration that backs that claim up. The 10-step pipeline visibly touches the tokeniser, the model, the BIO decoder, the KB, the taxonomy classifier, and the persistence + UI layer in one walkthrough. After slide 15, no panellist can credibly claim "but I only saw parts" — they have seen the parts compose end-to-end on a single article. Slide 15 is then the bridge into slide 16's evidence section ("with the system explained, we move to the evidence").

The function of slide 15. Two jobs. (1) Make the pipeline concrete — replace abstract architecture diagrams with one article going in and six colour-coded entity chips coming out. (2) Demonstrate fluency — the candidate's ability to walk through their own system end-to-end in 90 seconds without stumbling is itself the strongest signal of mastery. A candidate who stumbles on their own pipeline is, fairly or not, read as not having internalised it. Slide 15 is therefore the deck's most rehearsal-rewarding slide.

3 · The 10-step pipeline — fluency drill

This is the core skill on slide 15. The 10 steps want to be recitable as a list in under 15 seconds with no stumbling. The first rep is slow; the goal is to memorise the order so muscle memory takes over and the candidate is free to read the panel's reactions while speaking.

The 10-step list — memorise in order

  1. Article in
  2. Tokenise (WordPiece)
  3. BERT forward pass
  4. BIO decode to spans
  5. Confidence filter
  6. 5W1H grouping
  7. KB validate-and-enrich
  8. Taxonomy classify
  9. Persist
  10. Render chips

Drill protocol. Recite the list aloud five times, eyes closed. If you stumble on step 5 (where confidence-filter sits between BIO decode and 5W1H grouping), repeat the run from step 1. The stumble points are predictable: steps 4–6 (BIO decode → confidence filter → 5W1H group) and steps 7–8 (KB → taxonomy) are the two most common slip sites. Drill those pairs separately if needed.

The mnemonic. Group the 10 steps into four mental clusters: (1–2) intake — article in, tokenise · (3) model — BERT forward · (4–6) post-process — BIO decode, confidence filter, 5W1H group · (7–8) augment — KB validate-and-enrich, taxonomy classify · (9–10) deliver — persist, render chips. Four clusters, two-to-three items each. This is how the list stays in working memory without being read off the slide.

4 · The latency Pareto — three numbers, one engineering claim

The latency budget is one of the slide's signature elements. Three numbers — 75 / 15 / 10 — partition the ~150 ms inference budget. The candidate's job is to make the panel understand the budget without burning time on per-step millisecond math.

The Pareto partition (~150 ms total)

BERT forward · ~75 %
KB · ~15 %
~10 %
~112 ms · 110 M-param matrix multiplies on CPU
~22 ms · in-memory dict + alias match
~15 ms · all other steps combined
The engineering payoff. The 10 % "everything else" number is the slide's cleanest engineering claim. It includes seven of the ten steps — tokenise, BIO decode, confidence filter, 5W1H group, taxonomy classify, persist, render. Each is fast individually. The reason "everything else" sums to only 10 % is that the KB is in-memory. If the KB were a remote service, "KB lookup" alone would expand from ~22 ms to ~50 ms (network RTT + serialisation), and "everything else" would no longer feel free. The in-process architecture from slide 14 is what makes the Pareto land. Use that connection if you have room — it bridges slides 14 and 15 in one sentence.

6 · The three normalisation flourishes

The worked example carries three normalisation moments that distinguish VioNER from "just NER". Each turns raw extracted text into a structured value an analyst can query against. These three moments are what the candidate should pace deliberately during delivery — they are the slide's quietly-impressive engineering details.

Three normalisation moments — pace these deliberately

"Tuesday" → 2026-06-09
Relative dates resolved using the article's publication date as the anchor. If the article published on a Friday, "Tuesday" becomes that week's prior Tuesday. Without this, the analyst cannot range-query events by date.
"Mogadishu" → Somalia (SO)
CITY-level location resolved to COUNTRY via the KB's 200-city map. Without this, the analytics dashboard's per-country counts would all be empty for city-level extractions.
"twelve soldiers" → {12, "at least"}
CASUALTIES split into the integer count and a qualifier field. The qualifier preserves journalistic hedging ("at least 12", "as many as 30", "dozens") as structured data. Without this, analytics over casualties either over-states confidence or drops qualified counts entirely.
Why three flourishes and not more. The slide has exactly enough time for three flourishes, no more. Adding a fourth (KB enrichment of Al-Shabaab → canonical id, for instance) crosses into slide-13 territory and steals that slide's payoff. The three chosen here are mutually disjoint — date, location, casualty count — and together cover the three structural problems an unstructured-text NER would leave the analyst with. Three is the right number; resist adding more.

9 · Four pre-loaded interrupts

"Why is BERT 75 % of the latency? Is that not slow?"
"BERT-base has a hundred and ten million parameters. The forward pass involves matrix multiplications across all of them — that's the dominant cost. The other steps in the pipeline — tokenisation, BIO decode, confidence filter, KB lookup, 5W1H grouping, taxonomy classify, persist, render — are each comparatively trivial; together they're only about ten percent of the budget. A hundred and fifty milliseconds on CPU is well inside the operational target — analysts are not interactive on a per-keystroke basis; they paste, wait, read. Moving to GPU would push BERT below twenty milliseconds, but the deployment target was non-ML-engineers running this on standard server hardware, so CPU is the right baseline."
Three moves: (1) name the cost driver (110 M params); (2) name the residual cost (everything else is 10 %); (3) reframe "is that slow" as "is that fast enough" — operational fit, not theoretical maximum. Don't apologise for CPU inference; defend it as the deployment-target choice. The "paste, wait, read" cadence makes 150 ms feel fast because it is fast at the human-interaction level.
"What does 'normalised' actually mean for the date?"
"Relative dates — 'Tuesday', 'yesterday', 'last week' — are anchored to the article's publication date and resolved to a specific calendar date. If the article was published on Friday the thirteenth, 'Tuesday' becomes Tuesday the tenth — the prior Tuesday relative to publication. Absolute dates like 'on June 4th' are parsed directly into ISO format. Without this normalisation, the analytics layer cannot range-query events by date — 'show me all attacks in the first week of June' would miss every relatively-dated extraction. The publication date is metadata on the article, so it's available to the post-processor at extraction time."
Three moves: (1) name the two cases (relative + absolute); (2) give the worked example with a specific anchor (Friday the 13th → Tuesday the 10th); (3) name the downstream consequence (range-querying analytics). The third move is what converts "normalisation" from a technical detail to an operational requirement.
"How is the 'at least' qualifier preserved? Wouldn't most NER pipelines just extract '12'?"
"Most NER pipelines would extract '12' and lose the hedging. VioNER's post-processor parses the CASUALTIES span into a structured field — the integer count and a qualifier string. So 'at least twelve' becomes {killed: 12, qualifier: 'at least'}; 'as many as thirty' becomes {killed: 30, qualifier: 'as many as'}; 'dozens' becomes {killed: null, qualifier: 'dozens'}. Downstream consumers see both fields. This matters because casualty counts in journalism are systematically hedged — dropping the hedge either over-states confidence in the count or drops qualified extractions entirely. Both are common failure modes in conflict data."
The three example variants ('at least N' / 'as many as N' / 'dozens') show the breadth of the qualifier vocabulary the post-processor handles. The 'dozens' case — where the integer field is null — demonstrates the structured field can represent absence of a count. Naming the failure mode ("over-stating confidence or dropping qualified extractions") is what makes the qualifier-preservation feature visible as a design decision, not a coincidence.
"What happens if KB lookup fails or returns nothing?"
"The record is still persisted with the raw surface form. No fabrication. KB failure doesn't block extraction — that's one of the two guarantees from slide 13: validation never blocks; enrichment never invents. If the surface form 'Al-Shabaab fighters' doesn't match the KB, the actor field carries the raw string and the canonical-id field is empty. New armed groups not yet in the KB are exactly this case. The analyst sees the raw text and can flag the group for KB curation through the admin page."
Two moves: (1) state the behaviour (raw form persists); (2) cite the slide-13 guarantees by name. This is the slide that demonstrates "validation never blocks; enrichment never invents" operationally — when KB lookup is in the 10-step pipeline and the panel asks what happens at failure, the answer ties back to slide 13's hero line. Use this opportunity if the panel asks; it reinforces the cross-slide coherence of the deck.

12 · Quiz

Q1. The panel asks: "Why is BERT 75 % of the inference latency?"

Pick the strongest response.

  • "Transformers are inherently slow because of the self-attention mechanism's quadratic cost in sequence length."
  • "BERT was the slowest-performing model we tested; faster alternatives are available but were out of scope."
  • "BERT-base has 110 million parameters. The forward pass involves matrix multiplications across all of them on CPU — that's the dominant cost. The other steps (tokenisation, BIO decode, KB lookup) are comparatively trivial; together ~10 % of the budget. 150 ms on CPU is well inside the operational target — analysts paste, wait, read. CPU was the deployment-target choice."
  • "BERT spends most of its time on the [CLS] token's attention computation, which scales with vocabulary size."

Q2. What does "normalised to a calendar date" mean for the worked example's "Tuesday"?

Pick the correct meaning.

  • The string "Tuesday" is matched against a calendar lookup table and discarded if no match is found.
  • "Tuesday" is resolved to a specific ISO calendar date using the article's publication date as the anchor — if the article was published on a Friday, "Tuesday" becomes the previous Tuesday's date. This enables range-querying analytics by date.
  • The article is timestamped with the current server date, which is then compared against "Tuesday" to compute a delta.
  • "Tuesday" is converted to its day-of-week integer (1=Monday, 2=Tuesday) and stored as metadata.

Q3. The panel asks: "What happens if the KB lookup returns no match for an extracted actor?"

Pick the strongest response.

  • "The record is still persisted with the raw surface form. No fabrication. KB failure doesn't block extraction — slide 13's two guarantees: validation never blocks; enrichment never invents. New armed groups not yet in the KB are exactly this case; the analyst can flag for KB curation through the admin page."
  • "The pipeline routes the record to a fuzzy-matching fallback that picks the closest KB entry by string similarity."
  • "The record is discarded — without a canonical actor id, downstream analytics cannot aggregate it."
  • "The pipeline retries the KB lookup three times before failing the extraction with an error."

Q4. How is the casualty count "twelve soldiers" structured by the post-processor?

Pick the correct shape.

  • As the raw string "twelve soldiers" stored in the CASUALTIES field — no normalisation.
  • As the integer 12 only; the journalistic hedging ("at least") is dropped during span extraction.
  • As a single string "at least 12 soldiers killed" stored unparsed for later analyst processing.
  • As a structured field with the integer count and a qualifier string — {killed: 12, qualifier: 'at least'}. The qualifier preserves journalistic hedging as structured data, so downstream analytics doesn't over-state confidence or drop qualified extractions.
