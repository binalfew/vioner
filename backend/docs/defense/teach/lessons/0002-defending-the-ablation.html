Lesson 02 · Slide 18 — Ablation

Defend the ablation as a controlled experiment

Not as a result. The four numbers on the slide are evidence, not a claim — and rigorous evidence has to be defended as evidence.

Mission link. Survive the methodology questions on the loss contribution. Builds on. Lesson 01. Time. 15–20 minutes.

In Lesson 1 you learned to explain what focal loss is and why you used it. That gets you past tell me about your loss function. But the harder question — the one the methodology-minded panellist asks — is "how do you know your loss choice was better than the alternatives?". The answer is the §6.6 ablation table. This lesson teaches you to defend it.

1 · What an ablation actually is

An ablation study is a controlled experiment for software/ML methodology. You take your full system, hold everything constant, and turn one ingredient off at a time. The drop in performance is the contribution of that ingredient. It's the ML equivalent of "if I remove this component and the system still works the same, the component wasn't doing anything."

An ablation is rigorous when:

  • Identical conditions — same data, same training schedule, same random seeds, only the ingredient under test varies.
  • Multiple seeds — to confirm the result isn't a lucky run.
  • Statistical significance — the observed gain exceeds run-to-run noise by a comfortable margin.
  • Honest reporting — every cell of the table is reported, not just the ones that flatter the contribution.

The §6.6 table satisfies all four. This lesson teaches you how to demonstrate that under panel pressure.

2 · The table you'll defend

This is what your panel sees on slide 18. Memorise the four VICTIM numbers — they are the empirical core of the loss-function contribution.

Table 6.8 (§6.6). Per-entity F1 under four loss configurations. Identical data, identical schedule, identical seeds — only the loss function varies.
Loss configuration VICTIM F1 ACTION F1 DATE F1
Plain cross-entropy (baseline) 0.708 0.794 0.948
Weighted cross-entropy 0.776 0.825 0.953
Focal loss alone (γ = 2) 0.792 0.842 0.951
Focal + class weights (production) 0.817 0.866 0.956
Lift over baseline +10.9 +7.2 +0.8
Two things to point at while presenting. First — the bottom row (lift) on VICTIM and ACTION: the rare entities gained substantially. Second — the bottom row on DATE: no regression on the common entity. The combination doesn't trade off; it lifts the rare classes without sacrificing anything. That second point is what makes this a clean contribution.

This is what you say when you arrive at slide 18. Roughly one minute at calm pace. Section-by-section structured so even if interrupted mid-way, the key technical content has landed.

(Open) "The ablation in section 6.6 is the empirical defence of the loss-function contribution. Four configurations. Identical conditions. Only the loss changes."

(Walk the table) "Reading the VICTIM column. Plain cross-entropy: zero-point-seven-zero-eight. Weighted cross-entropy: zero-point-seven-seven-six. Focal loss alone: zero-point-seven-nine-two. Focal-plus-weights: zero-point-eight-one-seven."

(Headline) "Plus eleven F1 points over baseline on VICTIM. Plus seven-point-two on ACTION. No common-class regression — DATE went up zero-point-eight points."

(Rigour) "On significance: three random seeds, run-to-run variance plus-minus zero-point-four F1, so the eleven-point gain exceeds variance by twenty-five times. Paired bootstrap at the article level — p below zero-point-zero-one. The gain is real, not noise."

(Close) "This table is what makes the loss choice defensible as a contribution rather than an assertion."

4 · The complementarity inequality

The single most important conceptual claim of the ablation: the two ingredients complement each other. They are not redundant. If they were redundant, the production row would equal the better of the two ingredient-alone rows. It doesn't. It's higher.

$$\text{Focal + weights } (0.817) \;>\; \max\Bigl(\text{focal alone } (0.792), \text{ weighted CE } (0.776)\Bigr) \;=\; 0.792$$ The combination beats the better of the two ingredients by 2.5 F1 points. That's the evidence of complementarity.

In words: if class weights and focal loss were doing the same job, you'd see the combined row plateau at the better single-ingredient row. The fact that it lifts above that maximum means the two ingredients are addressing different parts of the imbalance problem — and the lesson 1 frame applies:

  • Class weights rebalance across classes (per-class multiplier on the loss).
  • Focal loss rebalances within a class (suppresses easy-correct tokens).

Different axes of the same imbalance problem → combination beats either alone → the table is the proof.

5 · Three predicted attacks

The methodology-minded panellist will pursue one of three lines. Pre-load the answers.

Attack 1. "How do you know this isn't just one lucky run?"
"Three random seeds per configuration. Run-to-run variance on macro F1 is plus-minus zero-point-four F1. The eleven-point VICTIM lift exceeds variance by about twenty-five times. We also ran a paired bootstrap at the article level — p below zero-point-zero-one. The gain is robust to noise."
The "25× headroom" framing is what makes this answer feel rigorous rather than hand-wavy. Land that number.
Attack 2. "Why exactly these four configurations? Could you be cherry-picking?"
"The four cells isolate the design space of the loss contribution: baseline, the class-weights ingredient alone, the focal-loss ingredient alone, and the combination. Each pairwise comparison answers a specific causal question. Adjacent design choices like the value of gamma, the class-weight cap, label smoothing — those are sensitivity analyses in section 5.5, not structural ablations. The §6.6 table answers what each loss-function ingredient contributes; sensitivity asks how robust the result is to parameter values. Different questions, different tables."
The "structural ablation vs sensitivity analysis" distinction is the key — it shows methodological literacy.
Attack 3. "Couldn't a third ingredient have outperformed this combination?"
"Possibly. We tested label smoothing with beta zero-point-one (Table 6.4) — marginal F1 effect, but better confidence calibration, so it's in the production loss. We did not test dice loss or effective-number weighting; those are documented as future-work item four. The contribution claim is specifically that focal loss plus class weights beats plain cross-entropy by a substantial margin — not that no alternative could ever do better. That's an honest, bounded claim."
"Honest, bounded claim" is the disarming move. It concedes the question's premise without conceding the contribution.

7 · Quiz

Q1. A panellist asks: "How do you know your VICTIM gain isn't just a lucky seed?"

Pick the most rigorous-sounding answer.

  • "We ran the experiment multiple times and got similar numbers."
  • "Three random seeds. Run-to-run variance is ±0.4 F1. The +11 VICTIM lift exceeds variance by 25×. Paired bootstrap at article level gives p < 0.01."
  • "VICTIM is a rare class so we expect bigger swings, but +11 is too big to be noise."
  • "The other entities also improved, so it's not just one fluke on VICTIM."

Q2. "Why these four configurations and not eight?"

Which framing best defends the table's scope?

  • "More cells would have made the table too large to fit on the slide."
  • "We did the structural ablation; adjacent ingredients would have been redundant."
  • "The four cells isolate the design space of the loss contribution. Adjacent choices like γ value, weight cap, label smoothing are sensitivity analyses in §5.5 — different question, different table."
  • "There are only four reasonable loss functions to compare against."

Q3. The complementarity inequality.

If focal loss and class weights were doing the same job (i.e. redundant), which row of the table would the production row equal?

  • The plain CE baseline row.
  • The better of the two ingredient-alone rows (focal alone = 0.792).
  • The sum of the two ingredient-alone improvements.
  • It would equal exactly one of the two ingredient-alone rows, but it's unclear which.

8 · The fluency checklist

Mentally tick each box. If you can't tick all six, do another rep of the section that broke down.

  • I can name all four loss configurations in order, from memory.
  • I can recite the four VICTIM numbers (0.708, 0.776, 0.792, 0.817) in under 15 seconds with no swaps.
  • I can deliver the 60-second read-aloud script without notes.
  • I can answer Attack 1 (statistical significance) with the "3 seeds, ±0.4 variance, 25× headroom, paired bootstrap p < 0.01" framing.
  • I can answer Attack 2 (why these four) with the "structural ablation vs sensitivity analysis" distinction.
  • I can state the complementarity inequality (0.817 > max(0.792, 0.776)) and explain what it proves.
If all six are ticked, you have moved from knowing the result to defending the evidence. That's what a methodology-minded panel is checking.
