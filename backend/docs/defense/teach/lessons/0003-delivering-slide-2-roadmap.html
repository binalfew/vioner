Lesson 03 · Slide 2 — Roadmap

Deliver the roadmap in 45 seconds

Slide 2 looks like a list. It isn't. It's where you frame the entire defence — and the integration thread you say here decides how every later slide gets received.

Mission link. Set the panel's frame in the first minute. Skill type. Presentation craft, not technical content. Time. 12 minutes.

Lessons 01 and 02 taught you to defend technical content. This lesson is different — it's about stagecraft. The roadmap slide is where you tell the panel what you're about to do and what frame to receive it in. Done well, every later slide lands more easily because you set up the context. Done badly, you sound like you're reading a table of contents.

1 · What's on the screen

This is what the panel sees. Notice it's a list — they can read it themselves. Your job is not to recite the list aloud. Your job is to tell them the shape and the thread.

Slide 2 of 22

Roadmap
  1. Problem & operational stakes (slides 3-5)
  2. Competitive landscape & the four gaps (slides 6-7)
  3. The solution & methodology (slides 8-9)
  4. The five contributions (slides 10-15)
  5. Evidence (slides 16-19)
  6. Limits, then close (slides 20-22)

2 · The 45-second script, in three beats

The script is two sentences. About 45 seconds at calm pace. Beat 1 names the arc. Beat 2 is silence. Beat 3 lands the through-line.

1
Beat 1 — the arc ~20 seconds
"This is the roadmap. I move from why the problem matters, to why existing tools don't close it, to the four contributions, then the evidence — model metrics, the ablation, KB impact, and user testing — and finally the limits and deployment path."
Notice: you're naming 6 sections in one breath, but in a single sentence with rhythm. The panel hears the shape — they don't need to hear "and then we'll cover X" six times.
PAUSE — half a second — eye contact with one panellist
2
Beat 2 — the strategic pause ~1 second
Don't speak. Make brief eye contact with one panel member. The pause says "and now I'm about to tell you the most important thing about this talk." A confident speaker takes pauses; a nervous one fills them.
Most novice presenters skip this. The pause is what makes Beat 3 land.
3
Beat 3 — the integration thread ~20 seconds
"One thread ties the whole talk together: each contribution closes one operational gap, and dropping any single piece breaks the chain. The contribution is the integration, not any one component."
End on "component." Clean stop. Don't trail. The next sound the panel hears should be your foot stepping toward the next slide, not "um" or "so".

3 · The one sentence that does all the work

Beat 3 is the strategic move of the entire defence. Memorise this exact phrasing — it's worth more than any other sentence in the talk.

The integration thread "The contribution is the integration, not any one component."

This twelve-word sentence neutralises three attacks before they happen:

Pre-empted attack 1. "Isn't this just engineering?" Answered: the integration is the contribution.
Pre-empted attack 2. "What's novel here?" Answered: the novelty is the combination, not any individual piece (each of which has precedent).
Pre-empted attack 3. "Could you have skipped component X?" Answered: no — dropping any piece breaks the chain.

By stating this frame in slide 2, every later slide that demonstrates an individual contribution becomes evidence for the integration claim, not a separate standalone claim that has to be defended on its own. Slide 2 is doing rhetorical work for slides 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 18 — it's the lever that makes the whole talk easier.

4 · The forbidden moves

Five things to never do on slide 2. Each one is the tell of a nervous student.

Don't read the list aloud "First we'll cover problem and stakes, then we'll discuss the competitive landscape, then we'll go through the four gaps, then…" — the panel can read. Tell them the shape, not the items.
Don't say "I'll talk about" six times Repeated "I'll talk about X" is what undergrads do. Use one verb for the whole arc, not six.
Don't trail off after the through-line "...integration, not any one component, so yeah, let me get started." Wrong. End on "component" and walk to the next slide. The silence after the sentence is your friend.
Don't apologise for the structure "It's a lot to cover, but I'll try to be quick." Cuts your authority. The arc is the arc; you don't apologise for it.
Don't go past 60 seconds At 45 seconds the panel is paying attention. At 60 they're zoning out. At 90 they've already started thinking about their first question. Keep moving.

5 · Timed practice — three reps

The whole script aloud, end-to-end, including the half-second pause between Beats 1 and 2. Target: 40 to 50 seconds. The timer below verifies.

0.0 s
How to fail gracefully if the panel interrupts. If a panellist raises a question DURING beat 1, finish your current clause and yield: "happy to take that — and I'll come back to the rest of the roadmap after." Then answer their question briefly and return to Beat 3 — the through-line is the only part you must deliver, regardless of what was interrupted.

6 · Quiz

Q1. What is Beat 3 ("the integration is the contribution") actually doing strategically?

Pick the best framing.

  • Setting expectations on talk length.
  • Summarising the conclusion the panel will hear at the end.
  • Pre-empting the three most likely attacks: "isn't this just engineering?", "what's novel?", and "could you have skipped component X?".
  • Telling the panel what each contribution is.

Q2. A panellist interrupts you halfway through Beat 1. What's the correct move?

Pick the most professional response.

  • Stop the roadmap entirely and answer their question, then move to slide 3.
  • Finish your current clause, yield politely, answer briefly, then return to deliver Beat 3 — the integration thread is non-negotiable.
  • Ask them to hold the question until the end of the talk.
  • Apologise for not being clearer and start the roadmap over.

Q3. The slide displays a 6-item list. Which is the correct way to deliver it?

Pick the strongest delivery instinct.

  • Read each item aloud so the panel doesn't have to.
  • Say "I'll cover items 1 through 6" to set the scope.
  • Skim it quickly and say "I'll just dive in" to save time.
  • Name the arc as one continuous sentence, then deliver the through-line. The panel reads the list themselves.

7 · Fluency checklist

Tick each box mentally. Don't move on until all six are ticked.

  • I can recite Beat 1 in one breath, in 18-22 seconds, without looking at the slide.
  • I can hold the half-second pause between Beat 1 and Beat 3 without filling it with "um".
  • I can recite Beat 3 verbatim — including "the contribution is the integration, not any one component."
  • I can finish on "component" with a clean stop and walk to slide 3 without trailing words.
  • The total run is between 40 and 55 seconds in my last timed rep.
  • I can explain to a friend in one sentence WHY Beat 3 is the most strategic sentence in the talk.
If all six are ticked, you have a roadmap delivery that primes the rest of the talk. From slide 3 onward, every contribution you describe becomes evidence FOR the integration claim — which is exactly the frame you want the panel operating in.

Where to go next

When you're ready, ask:

  • "Teach me what to say on slide 1 — the title slide." (First impressions, opening line, posture.)
  • "Teach me what to say on slide 22 — the close." (Restating the five contributions, the final ask, eye contact.)
  • "Drill me cold on slide 2 — I'll recite from memory and you tell me where I drifted."
  • "Teach me the panel-interrupt protocol in detail." (How to handle questions mid-slide gracefully.)
