Lesson 05 · Slide 3 — Operational stakes

Make the problem feel real in 75 seconds

Slide 3 is where you say five numbers and six institutional acronyms in a row, smoothly, and land "this is not an academic curiosity" — the line that establishes mission relevance.

Mission link. Convince the panel the problem is operationally real. Skill type. Number + acronym delivery under density. Time. 15 minutes.

Lesson 4 and lesson 3 taught you to frame the talk in slides 1 and 2. Slide 3 is the first content slide — the moment where you stop talking about the talk and start talking about the problem. Most defences die here because the candidate stumbles on a number or scrambles an acronym, signalling unfamiliarity with the domain. This lesson is specifically about saying dense numerical and institutional content cleanly.

The delivery trap. Reading the slide is tempting but wrong. The panel can read four numbers and six acronyms themselves. Your job is to narrate them — give them rhythm, weight, and the strategic conclusion. The slide is the backdrop; you are the foreground.

2 · The 75-second script, in two beats

Two paragraphs. The first lands the numbers; the second names the institutional consumers and delivers the strategic line.

1
Beat 1 — the four numbers ~30 seconds
"The operational stakes. About thirty thousand African violent events are reported and coded each year. Twenty-plus active armed conflicts in any given year. All fifty-four countries tracked, roughly thirty with mass-casualty events in 2024."
Four numbers in 30 seconds. Each one needs to land — see Section 3 on number chunking. Don't list them like a phone number; pause briefly after each so it registers.
2
Beat 2 — institutional consumers + strategic line ~40 seconds
"This is the operational backbone for the AU Peace and Security Council, the AU early-warning system, ECOWAS, IGAD, UN OCHA, and UNHCR. The point I want to land: this is not an academic curiosity — reduced extraction cost benefits every one of those consumers."
Six named consumers in one breath. See Section 4 on acronym pronunciation and list pacing. End on "consumers" with a clean stop and advance to slide 4.

3 · The number-delivery technique

Four numbers in 30 seconds sounds fast. It isn't if you chunk them right. The key is to use natural English number-words ("thirty thousand", not "three-zero-comma-zero-zero-zero") and to land each one with a tiny pause.

Don't say "30,000 African violent events 20-plus active armed conflicts 54 countries tracked roughly 30 with mass-casualty" Say it like this "About thirty thousand / African violent events / are reported and coded each year. // (breath) // Twenty-plus / active armed conflicts / in any given year. // (breath) // All fifty-four countries tracked, roughly thirty with mass-casualty events in 2024."

The chunk-then-breathe rhythm

  1. Each number gets its own clause. Don't pile two numbers into one sentence.
  2. Pause between sentences. A half-second is enough to let the number register without sounding awkward.
  3. Say numbers as words. "Thirty thousand" beats "30 thousand" or worst-case "thirty-K".
  4. Slightly slower than your normal cadence. Numbers are dense — your brain knows them so well you'll speed up. Force yourself to slow.

4 · The acronym-delivery technique

Beat 2 has six institutional consumers in one sentence. Two are spelled out (AU-PSC and AU-CEWS become "AU Peace and Security Council" and "AU early-warning system"); four are spoken as acronyms (ECOWAS, IGAD, UN OCHA, UNHCR). This is deliberate — pure acronym soup ("AUPSC, AUCEWS, ECOWAS, IGAD, UNOCHA, UNHCR") would lose the panel by acronym three.

In the script as Pronounce as Stands for
AU Peace and Security Council "AY-yoo Peace and Security Council" African Union peace body (AU-PSC)
AU early-warning system "AY-yoo early-warning system" AU Continental Early Warning System (AU-CEWS)
ECOWAS "EE-koh-wass" (word, not letters) Economic Community of West African States
IGAD "EYE-gad" (word, not letters) Intergovernmental Authority on Development
UN OCHA "U-N OH-cha" (mixed) UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs
UNHCR "U-N-H-C-R" (letters) UN High Commissioner for Refugees

The list-of-six pacing rule

Saying six consumers in a row sounds robotic if you give each one the same beat. Use this rhythm instead:

  1. Cluster 1 — slowly: "AU Peace and Security Council, AU early-warning system" — two full names, give each one weight. ~6 seconds.
  2. Tiny pause. Half a second. Lets the AU names register.
  3. Cluster 2 — flowing: "ECOWAS, IGAD, UN OCHA, and UNHCR" — four short ones in a list rhythm. ~4 seconds.
  4. The "and" before UNHCR matters. It signals "end of list" and lets you breathe before the strategic line.
If you mispronounce one — keep going. Don't correct yourself mid-sentence. If you say "ee-cow-ass" instead of "ee-koh-wass", the panel doesn't care. Stopping to correct it draws attention and undermines composure. Plow forward to the next acronym.

5 · The hero line — what beat 2 is doing strategically

The whole reason for naming six institutional consumers is to set up this single sentence:

The strategic line — memorise verbatim "This is not an academic curiosity — reduced extraction cost benefits every one of those consumers."

This twelve-word line is doing three jobs at once:

  • Pre-empts the "is this useful?" question. Panel members at AAU CS often ask "so what?" — this line answers it before they ask.
  • Names the mechanism of benefit. Not "would help" or "could be useful" — specifically reduced extraction cost. That's the operational lever VioNER pulls.
  • Closes the loop with the named consumers. By saying "every one of those consumers", you're invoking the institutional list you just delivered. They're not generic users — they're a specific, real list of bodies that already exist.

6 · If pressed on the 30,000 figure

The most likely interrupt during slide 3 is "where does the 30,000 number come from?". Have this answer pre-loaded.

"Where does the thirty thousand figure come from?"
"That's ACLED's African coverage — consistent at twenty-eight to thirty-five thousand every year since twenty-twenty. The order of magnitude is what matters; the exact number varies slightly by data year."
Land "ACLED" cleanly — it's the answer's keyword. The 28-35k range concedes precision without conceding the order-of-magnitude claim, which is what the slide depends on.

8 · Quiz

Q1. Why does the script spell out "AU Peace and Security Council" but leave "ECOWAS" as an acronym?

Pick the strongest reason.

  • Because AU-PSC is a less well-known acronym than ECOWAS.
  • Because the script writer prefers the full names.
  • Six pure acronyms in one sentence overloads the panel. Spelling out the first two AU bodies — both unfamiliar to non-specialists — preserves comprehension; the four well-known regional bodies stay as acronyms.
  • Because AU-PSC and AU-CEWS are not yet stabilised as common acronyms.

Q2. A panellist asks you mid-slide: "Where does the 30k figure come from?"

Pick the best one-sentence answer.

  • "It's a rough estimate based on recent reporting trends."
  • "That's ACLED's African coverage — consistent at 28 to 35 thousand every year since 2020. The order of magnitude is what matters; the exact number varies slightly by data year."
  • "It comes from public conflict-tracking data."
  • "I derived it from the African Union peace and security reports."

Q3. Which sentence on slide 3 is doing the most strategic work?

Pick the line you cannot afford to fluff.

  • "About thirty thousand African violent events are reported and coded each year."
  • "This is the operational backbone for the AU Peace and Security Council..."
  • "All fifty-four countries tracked, roughly thirty with mass-casualty events in 2024."
  • "This is not an academic curiosity — reduced extraction cost benefits every one of those consumers."

9 · Fluency checklist

Tick each item mentally. The dense content of slide 3 punishes lazy preparation — drill until every box is honest.

  • I can say "about thirty thousand African violent events" without saying "30 thousand" or "30K".
  • I can deliver the four numbers (30k, 20+, 54, 30) in order from memory, no slide prompt.
  • I can pronounce ECOWAS as "EE-koh-wass" and IGAD as "EYE-gad" — as words, not letters.
  • I can name all six institutional consumers in the right order, in one breath each cluster.
  • I can land "this is not an academic curiosity" with conviction, not as a throwaway.
  • I have the "where does the 30k come from?" answer pre-loaded and can deliver it in 10 seconds.
  • My last timed rep was between 70 and 90 seconds.
If all seven are ticked, slide 3 — which kills many defences with number-stumbles and acronym-scrambles — is armoured. The panel hears institutional fluency, which signals "this candidate knows the operational landscape." That signal carries through the next ten slides.
