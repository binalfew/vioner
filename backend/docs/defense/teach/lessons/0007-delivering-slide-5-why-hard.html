Lesson 07 · Slide 5 — Why this is hard

Deliver four challenges in 75 seconds — without sounding like a robot counting

Slide 5 is the bridge: from "the problem is operationally real" (slides 3-4) to "and here's why no one has solved it" (sets up slides 10-15). The delivery skill is parallel-structure enumeration with varied internal weight.

Mission link. Establish technical hardness; pre-empt "why hasn't anyone solved this?" Skill type. Parallel-structure delivery + named-entity pronunciation. Time. 14 minutes.

Lesson 6 closed the problem case with the labour-cost arithmetic and the review-not-replace framing. Slide 5 does the next move: enumerates four reasons why this problem is technically hard. Each challenge previews a contribution that comes later — domain entities → KB (slide 13), class imbalance → focal loss (slide 12), geographic ambiguity → confusion matrix (slide 20), 5W1H grouping → schema (slide 10). Your delivery should hint at "this matters later" without revealing the solutions yet.

The delivery risk on this slide. Four numbered challenges is the most dangerous structure in presenting — it's the easiest place to fall into mechanical "one, two, three, four" cadence. The slide visually invites you to read off bullets. Your job is to make each challenge feel like a distinct observation with its own weight, not item N of a list.

2 · The 75-second script, in five beats

One opener plus four challenge beats. Each challenge is ~15 seconds; the opener is ~10. Total target: 70 seconds in delivery, allowing breathing room.

0
Opener ~10 seconds
"Why automating this is harder than generic NER. Four things."
"Four things" is the most important phrase in the whole slide. Land it with a slight pause after. The pause signals "I am about to enumerate; track with me."
1
Challenge 1 — Domain entities ~17 seconds
"One — domain entities. Generic models tag Al-Shabaab as a generic ORGANIZATION, don't know JNIM or RSF, and the African armed-group landscape is dynamic — groups splinter, rebrand, recombine."
Three armed-group names in one sentence — pronunciation matters (see Section 4). End with the "splinter, rebrand, recombine" triplet for rhythm.
2
Challenge 2 — Class imbalance ~17 seconds
"Two — severe class imbalance. Seventy-eight percent of tokens are non-entity O, and the operationally critical entities — victim, action, casualties — are the rarest, just a few percent each."
The 78% number is the weight of this challenge. Slow it down slightly: "seventy-eight percent" not "78%". The three rarest entities — say them like a triplet, each gets a beat.
3
Challenge 3 — Geographic ambiguity ~15 seconds
"Three — geographic ambiguity. "Fighting in Goma" could be city, district, or region. This is why DISTRICT is the weakest entity in the final results."
The Goma quote is the example that makes the abstract problem concrete. Quote it like a quote — slight vocal lift. Then drop into the consequence: "DISTRICT is the weakest entity."
4
Challenge 4 — 5W1H + multi-event ~18 seconds
"Four — 5W1H grouping with perpetrator-versus-victim distinction is not standard NER output. And multi-event articles pack several incidents into one piece, which means the model has to segment as well as extract."
This is the longest item because it has two sub-points. The "And" mid-sentence is the pivot — pause briefly before "And multi-event articles". End on "segment as well as extract" with a clean stop. Advance to slide 6.

3 · How to enumerate four items without sounding mechanical

The default failure mode of slide 5 is reading "One: domain entities. Two: class imbalance. Three: geographic ambiguity. Four: 5W1H grouping." with identical pace and pitch. The panel hears a list and zones out. The fix: each item has internally different shape, and you deliver each with the cadence matched to that shape.

Item Internal shape Delivery cadence
One Topic + three named examples + a triplet ("splinter, rebrand, recombine") Steady. Names get small emphasis. End on the triplet rhythm.
Two Topic + a statistic (78%) + three rarest entities Slow on the statistic. The number is the weight; let it land.
Three Topic + a quote example + a consequence claim Lift on the quote ("Fighting in Goma"), drop on the consequence.
Four Topic + a second sub-point introduced with "And" Pause before "And". This is the longest item — give it room.

The dash, not the colon

Say "One — domain entities" with a half-beat dash, not "One: domain entities" with a hard colon. The dash creates a natural pause that signals "here comes the topic"; the colon creates a robotic gap that signals "I am reading a list."

The four-item rhythm: build, peak, descend, complete

  • Item 1 builds — names and examples, sets up the pattern.
  • Item 2 peaks — the 78% number is the heaviest single fact on the slide. Let it carry weight.
  • Item 3 descends — the Goma quote is light and concrete; you're easing the panel.
  • Item 4 completes — the longest item, the most subtle, the "and" mid-item adds a final beat before you transition.

4 · Named-entity pronunciation

Five named entities appear in this slide. Mispronouncing any of them signals unfamiliarity with the domain — fatal on the slide that's supposed to demonstrate your grasp of the African conflict landscape.

Why pronounce ORGANIZATION distinctly. The script says "generic models tag Al-Shabaab as a generic ORGANIZATION" — and the panel needs to hear that ORGANIZATION is a tag-name, not a regular word. Slow it down by one beat and treat it like a technical term.

5 · Forward references — each challenge previews a contribution

Slide 5 is doing a second job behind the visible enumeration: each challenge sets up a contribution the panel will see later. You don't deliver the contribution on slide 5 — you just plant the problem so the contribution lands when it appears.

6 · Likely panel interrupts

"Aren't there pre-trained African NER models that handle some of this?"
"Masakhane and AfriBERTa are excellent for African-language coverage, but neither addresses violent-event-specific entities like perpetrator-versus-victim role distinction or the African armed-group landscape with KB validation. They handle some of the language coverage; they don't close the four operational gaps. Slide six has the landscape comparison."
Name the existing work specifically (Masakhane, AfriBERTa) — concession to expertise. Pivot to "they don't close the four operational gaps" — your differentiator.
"Is 78% really the O-token share? That seems high."
"Seventy-eight percent on our training corpus, computed from the BIO label distribution. It's typical for span-based NER on news text — many tokens are punctuation, function words, or filler. The full distribution is in section five-point-three and table six-point-five reports the class-weight values derived from it."
Cite the section. The 78% is a calculated number, not an estimate.
"Why is DISTRICT specifically hard, given you said the model is otherwise strong?"
"Many African districts share names with their main city or with their region. Goma is the canonical example — city, district capital, and regional centre all simultaneously. The model defaults to CITY because that's more often the correct answer on training data, producing a consistent pattern of confusion. Slide twenty shows the confusion matrix; future-work item four is a span-level CRF that would add label-to-label constraints."
"Goma" is the operational shorthand. Use it. Concede the limitation; offer the future-work direction.

7 · Timed practice

Target: 65-85 seconds for the full opener + four challenges. The pacing variation (slow on 78%, lift on Goma, pause before "And") naturally extends the total.

0.0 s
The harder rep. Cover the slide visual. Now recite the four challenges in order — "one, two, three, four" — without referring to the slide. The challenge titles are: domain entities · class imbalance · geographic ambiguity · 5W1H grouping with multi-event. If you swap "domain entities" with "geographic ambiguity" or forget that challenge 4 has two sub-points, the panel notices the slip — drill until you can hit all four titles in order, every time.

8 · Quiz

Q1. The most dangerous delivery failure on slide 5 is:

Pick the failure mode this lesson is designed to prevent.

  • Mispronouncing JNIM or Al-Shabaab.
  • Going over the 75-second time budget.
  • Reading "one, two, three, four" with identical pace and pitch, turning the enumeration into a mechanical checklist.
  • Forgetting to mention the 78% statistic.

Q2. When you say "this is why DISTRICT is the weakest entity in the final results", what are you strategically doing?

Pick the right reading.

  • Apologising for a weakness in the model.
  • Pre-announcing a result the panel will see on slide 20 — owning the limitation 14 minutes before they would otherwise notice it.
  • Asking the panel to forgive imperfect performance on DISTRICT.
  • Signalling that DISTRICT was the hardest entity to train.

Q3. Why does challenge 4 have an "And" mid-sentence (introducing multi-event articles)?

Pick the strongest reason.

  • It signals a second sub-point within challenge 4 — the model has to segment AND extract, not just one. The pause before "And" gives the second sub-point its own weight without making it a fifth bullet on a four-item slide.
  • It's a grammatical filler with no strategic role.
  • It connects multi-event articles to the previous three challenges.
  • It's there because the script writer wanted to slow the closing pace.

9 · Fluency checklist

  • I deliver the opener — "Four things." — with a half-second pause that signals "enumeration coming."
  • I pronounce Al-Shabaab as "al-sha-BAAB", JNIM as "JEE-nim", and Goma as "GOH-ma" without hesitation.
  • I say "seventy-eight percent" slower than the surrounding words — it's the heaviest fact on the slide.
  • I quote "Fighting in Goma" with a vocal lift and drop on the consequence claim.
  • I pause briefly before the "And" in challenge 4 so the multi-event sub-point gets its own weight.
  • I do NOT reveal which contribution closes each challenge — I plant the problem, let it land later.
  • My last timed rep was between 65 and 85 seconds with internal pace variation.
If all seven are ticked, slide 5 lands as four distinct technical observations, not a recited checklist. The panel hears "this person actually understands why this problem is hard". That impression carries through every contribution slide — when you reveal the KB, focal loss, confusion matrix, and schema later, each one feels earned.
