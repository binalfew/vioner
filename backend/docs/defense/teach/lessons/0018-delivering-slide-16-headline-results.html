3 · The F1 primer — own every term before the panel asks

Slide 16 is the slide where a panellist is most likely to test whether you actually understand your own metric. The full chain — TP/FP/FN → precision → recall → F1 → micro vs macro — must be reproducible on a whiteboard. Here is the whole chain with VioNER's numbers.

The three buckets, then two ratios, then one number

True PositiveModel predicted an entity span and it exactly matches a gold span — right type, exact boundaries.
False PositiveModel predicted a span that matches no gold span — a false alarm the analyst must delete.
False NegativeA gold span the model failed to find — a miss the analyst must add by hand.
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP) — "of the spans I predicted, what fraction were right?"
RecallTP / (TP + FN) — "of the spans that exist, what fraction did I find?"
F1Harmonic mean of the two. High only when both are high — 100 % precision with 10 % recall scores ~0.18, not 0.55. The harmonic mean punishes one-sided performance.
F1  =  2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall) harmonic mean — punishes imbalance between the two

Worked example — 100 gold VICTIM spans (whiteboard-ready)

Setup100 gold VICTIMs · model predicts 90 spans as VICTIM
BucketsTP = 82 · FP = 8 (false alarms) · FN = 18 (missed)
Precision82 / 90 = 0.911 — "when it said VICTIM, right 91 % of the time"
Recall82 / 100 = 0.820 — "of the actual victims, found 82 %"
F12 × 0.911 × 0.820 / (0.911 + 0.820) = 0.863
Readingout of 100 real victims: find ~82, with ~8 false alarms
Why this worked example matters. The operational reading in the last line — "find 82, with 8 false alarms" — is the translation move the whole evidence section depends on. An F1 number is abstract; "82 found, 8 false alarms per 100" is what an analyst experiences. If a panellist ever looks unconvinced by an F1 number, drop to this concrete reading. It works for every per-entity number on slide 17 too.

4 · Micro vs macro — the two-point gap and what it means

The slide reports both averages, and the panel may ask why they differ. The answer requires knowing how each is computed — and the difference is a one-sentence structural fact about VioNER's per-entity distribution.

Micro (pooled TP/FP/FN)190,0750.909
The one-sentence explanation. Macro averages the eight per-entity F1s equally — VICTIM's 0.817 counts as much as DATE's 0.956. Micro pools all TP/FP/FN counts first, so high-support entities dominate. Micro comes out two points higher (0.909 vs 0.887) because the three highest-support entities — ACTOR, CITY, DATE — also happen to have the three highest F1s. That relationship is good news: the entities the analyst sees most often are the ones the model handles best. If the relationship were inverted (rare entities strong, common entities weak), micro would fall below macro — a warning sign.
When to use which — the deployment heuristic. Macro answers "does the model treat all entities fairly?" — it's the fairness number, and it's the number the ablation on slide 18 uses (rare-class gains move macro most). Micro answers "what fraction of entities in a batch will I get right?" — it's the throughput number, and it's the headline because it estimates the analyst's actual experience. Reporting both, with micro > macro explained, is itself an honesty signal.

5 · The honest-metric reframe — the core skill

Slide 16's transferable craft is the honest-metric reframe: when your slide contains a high number that is misleading and a lower number that is honest, you demote the high number before anyone asks. The token-accuracy demotion is the canonical instance.

The three-move honest-metric reframe

1Name the tempting number and its trap. "Token accuracy is 96.7 % — but 78 % of tokens are O." The trap is structural: the metric is inflated by the majority class.
2Run the degenerate-model test. "A model that predicts O everywhere already scores 78 % accuracy and learns nothing." The degenerate model is the audit: any metric a know-nothing model scores well on is not measuring learning.
3Name the honest metric and why it survives the test. "F1 ignores correct O predictions entirely — it measures only how well actual entities are recovered. The degenerate model scores zero." F1 survives the audit; that's why it leads.
The trap. Do not let the panel discover the accuracy inflation themselves. If a panellist works out "wait — 78 % of tokens are O, so accuracy is mostly free" before you've said it, the 96.7 % reads as an attempted inflation and every other number on the slide inherits the suspicion. Demoting your own best-looking number is the single highest-credibility move available on this slide — it buys trust that slides 17 and 18 spend.

This is the deck's honest-middle pattern applied to metrics. Slides 9–14 named and refused dismissive readings of contributions; slide 16 names and refuses an inflated reading of the candidate's own results. Same posture, new target: the candidate polices their own numbers harder than the panel would.

6 · The benchmark-contextualisation move

The second craft skill: answering "is 0.909 actually good?" without over-claiming. The move has a fixed shape — name the canonical benchmark, name the difficulty delta, then claim exactly as much as the comparison supports.

CoNLL-2003 (the canonical benchmark)

~0.92

4 entities (PER, ORG, LOC, MISC) · general news · mild imbalance · decades of tuning by the whole field

VioNER

0.909

8 entities · violent-event domain · severe imbalance (78 % O, rarest class < 3 % of spans) · one thesis

The exact claim to make — no more, no less. "Competitive on a harder distribution." Not "better than CoNLL" (false — 0.909 < 0.92). Not "state of the art" (unverifiable). The claim is that VioNER is within about a point of the canonical benchmark while solving a problem with twice the entity types and far worse imbalance. The comparison's job is to give the panel a reference frame, not to win a leaderboard. If pressed harder, backup slide B9 has the direct comparison: on the four entity types shared with generic BERT NER, VioNER scores 0.94 vs 0.82 macro — but that number stays in reserve.

7 · Strict span-level scoring — the honesty footnote that earns trust

The final element on the slide: "all numbers are strict span-level — exact boundaries and type — reported for honesty." This one sentence does disproportionate work.

Strict vs relaxed — what the choice means

Strict matchA predicted span counts as TP only if both the entity type and the exact boundaries match the gold span. "12 civilians" predicted against gold "at least 12 civilians" = FP and FN. CoNLL convention.
Relaxed matchPartial overlap (e.g. 50 %) counts as a hit. Scores come out 1.5–2 F1 points higher on this data.
Why strictA partial-match span doesn't save analyst time — they still have to edit the boundary. Strict matches what the consumer actually experiences. Relaxed would inflate the number without inflating the usefulness.
Note the pattern repeating. This is the honest-metric reframe a second time on the same slide: the candidate had a free +1.5–2 F1 available by choosing relaxed scoring and declined it, and says so. Two self-demotions on one slide (accuracy → F1, relaxed → strict) is what makes slide 16 the deck's credibility anchor. Both demotions share the same justification: report the number that corresponds to the consumer's experience.

11 · Four pre-loaded interrupts

"Why is your headline metric F1 and not accuracy? 96.7 % sounds better."
"Because seventy-eight percent of tokens are O — non-entity. A degenerate model that predicts O everywhere already scores seventy-eight percent token accuracy without learning anything. F1 ignores correct O predictions entirely and measures only how well the model recovers actual entities — on F1, that degenerate model scores zero. For an extraction task, what matters is the entities you found, not the non-entities you correctly ignored. That's why F1 leads and accuracy is the supporting number."
The degenerate-model test is the whole answer. Note the symmetry that closes it: the degenerate model scores 78 % on accuracy and 0.0 on F1 — the two metrics maximally disagree about a model that learned nothing, and F1 is the one telling the truth. The 78 % figure deliberately echoes slide 12's imbalance motivation — same number, two slides, one coherent story.
"Why is micro F1 higher than macro? Shouldn't they be the same?"
"They'd only be the same if every entity had identical F1. Micro pools all the true-positive, false-positive, and false-negative counts before computing F1, so high-support entities dominate — ACTOR has forty-seven thousand spans against VICTIM's five and a half thousand. Macro averages the eight per-entity F1s equally, so VICTIM's 0.817 weighs the same as DATE's 0.956. Micro comes out two points higher because the highest-support entities also have the highest F1. That's actually the reassuring direction — the entities analysts encounter most often are the ones the model is best at. The inverted relationship would be the warning sign."
Four moves: define both computations, give the support contrast (47 k vs 5.5 k), explain the direction of the gap, then reframe the gap as good news. The last move is the one most candidates miss — the two-point gap is not an embarrassment to explain away but a structural property to read out loud.
"How can I trust these numbers aren't inflated by data leakage between train and validation?"
"Three defences, applied in order. The split is article-level, not sentence-level — sentences from the same article never appear on both sides. Hash-based deduplication ran before the split, so near-duplicate wire copies can't straddle it. And the augmentation template pools were partitioned — templates used to generate training examples were never used for validation examples. Section 6.13 of the thesis lists the residual leakage risks honestly — including that wire-service stories about the same event with different wording can still appear on both sides, which is a limitation of any news corpus."
The three defences in order of strength: article-level split (the structural one), hash dedup (the near-duplicate one), template partition (the augmentation-specific one — the defence the panel won't expect you to have thought of). Then the §6.13 concession, volunteered. Conceding the residual wire-copy risk before being pushed is the leakage version of the honest-metric reframe.
"Is a single training run enough? These could be a lucky seed."
"The thesis ran each configuration with three random seeds — forty-two, seventeen, ninety-one. Run-to-run macro F1 variance is about plus-or-minus zero-point-four percentage points. The headline numbers are robust at that scale, and every gain reported in the slide-18 ablation exceeds that variance by an order of magnitude — the eleven-point VICTIM gain is roughly twenty-five times the seed noise. Paired bootstrap at the article level confirms significance for the rare-entity gains. Sections 6.4 and 6.6 carry the details."
Name the seeds (it shows the runs really happened), name the variance (±0.4 pp), then use the variance to pre-arm slide 18 — the ablation gain vs seed noise ratio (25×) is the line that kills the "lucky seed" attack permanently. Mentioning "+11" here in the ratio context is acceptable since the panellist forced the reliability question; do not expand into the ablation table itself.

12 · The hero close

The hero line — memorise verbatim "I lead with F1, not accuracy — seventy-eight percent of tokens are O, so a model that predicts O everywhere already scores seventy-eight percent accuracy and learns nothing."

Why this line is the hero

  • It demotes your own best-looking number. The 96.7 % is the biggest number on the slide and you are the one explaining why it doesn't matter. No panel attack on metric choice survives this — you made the attack first, better.
  • The degenerate model is a self-contained proof. "Predicts O everywhere → 78 % accuracy → learned nothing" is a three-step argument any CS panellist completes in their head before you finish the sentence. It doesn't ask for trust; it demonstrates.
  • It re-uses the deck's central number. 78 % O is the imbalance figure from slide 12 — the same fact that motivated focal loss and class weights now motivates the metric choice. One number, two duties, deck-wide coherence.
  • Consistent with the deck's posture. Slides 9–14 each named and refused a dismissive reading of a contribution. Slide 16 turns the same discipline inward: honest metric, not flattering metric. The candidate polices their own numbers harder than the panel would — which is precisely what makes the numbers believable.

14 · Quiz

Q1. The panel asks: "Why F1 and not accuracy? Your accuracy is 96.7 %."

Pick the strongest response.

  • "F1 is the standard metric in the NER literature, so we follow the convention for comparability."
  • "78 % of tokens are O — a degenerate model that predicts O everywhere already scores 78 % accuracy and learns nothing. F1 ignores correct O predictions and measures only recovery of actual entities; the degenerate model scores zero on it. For extraction, what matters is the entities found, not the non-entities correctly ignored."
  • "Accuracy and F1 measure roughly the same thing; F1 is just more granular for multi-class problems."
  • "Accuracy is reported too — 96.7 % — so both views are available; F1 simply leads the slide."

Q2. Why is micro F1 (0.909) higher than macro F1 (0.887)?

Pick the correct structural explanation.

  • Micro F1 uses relaxed span matching while macro uses strict matching, adding roughly two points.
  • Micro F1 is computed on the training set and macro on the validation set, so micro benefits from memorisation.
  • Micro pools TP/FP/FN across entities so high-support entities dominate; macro weights all eight equally. The gap is positive because the highest-support entities (ACTOR 47.6 k, CITY 44.4 k, DATE 31.9 k spans) also have the highest F1s — the reassuring direction, since analysts see those entities most often.
  • Macro F1 includes the O class in its average, which drags it down relative to micro.

Q3. The panel asks: "How do I know these numbers aren't inflated by train/validation leakage?"

Pick the strongest response.

  • "The validation set was held out from the start, so leakage is impossible by construction."
  • "We used an 80/20 random split, which is the standard practice in the field."
  • "Leakage would inflate all entities equally, but our per-entity F1s vary widely — so there can't be leakage."
  • "Three defences: article-level split (sentences from one article never straddle the split), hash-based deduplication before splitting, and augmentation template pools partitioned between train and validation. §6.13 lists the residual risks honestly — including same-event wire copies with different wording."

Q4. What does "strict span-level scoring" mean, and why use it when relaxed scoring adds 1.5–2 F1 points?

Pick the correct answer.

  • A prediction counts as TP only if both entity type and exact boundaries match the gold span — CoNLL convention. Relaxed (partial-overlap) scoring would score higher but is operationally misleading: a partial-match span doesn't save analyst time, they still have to edit it. Strict matches what the consumer actually experiences.
  • Strict scoring requires the model's confidence to exceed a fixed threshold before a span is counted, which filters out low-quality predictions.
  • Strict scoring evaluates each token independently rather than at span level, making it more granular and therefore more honest.
  • Strict scoring was required because the validation set's gold annotations only mark exact boundaries, leaving no choice in the matter.
