Lesson 11 · Slide 9

Methodology: Design science + three iteration loops

The methodological hinge. Where the talk pivots from "what's wrong with what exists" to "what VioNER does" — and where every contribution that follows gets its rigour pre-stamped.
Slide: 9 of 22 Target time: 90 seconds Hero line: "Measurement, not preference" Reserved cards: κ = 0.78 · 212k → 50k · +11 F1

1 · Where this slide sits in the arc

Slides 1-8 built up the problem: stakes, bottleneck, technical hardness, landscape, four gaps, the five-component preview. Slide 9 is the methodological hinge — it pre-stamps every contribution that follows (slides 10-14) with a research-method label. Without this slide, the panel could later ask "how do we know any of these were principled choices?". With this slide, the answer is already on record: each was driven by an iteration loop closed by a measured signal.

The function of slide 9. It buys research-level legitimacy for the five contributions that follow. By minute 12 of the talk you've said the words "design science" out loud and shown three measured iteration cycles. Everything after this slide inherits that rigour — the contributions are no longer assertions, they're outputs of a methodology.

What changes after slide 9

  • The panel's framing shifts. Up to slide 8 they're listening for "is this a real problem?" From slide 10 onward they're listening for "is each contribution principled?" Slide 9 sets up the second posture.
  • The "is this just engineering?" attack gets pre-empted. By naming the methodology and demonstrating three iterations, slide 9 makes the attack land flat — the answer is already on the screen.
  • The reserved numbers (κ=0.78, 212k→50k, +11 F1) become inheritance for later slides. They appear here only in reserve; when slides 11, 12, 18 deploy them with full context, they feel pre-validated rather than freshly minted.

4 · "Measurement, not preference" — the seven-word hinge

This is the rhetorical move that makes design science readable as a methodology rather than a label. Without this line, the three loops sound like sequential decisions a developer might describe in a sprint retro. With this line, they become evidence that the candidate iterated on data, schema, and loss because measured signals told them to, not because of taste.

If the panel hears "preference"

"I chose to oversample rare classes because it felt right."

"I narrowed the schema because 26 categories seemed unwieldy."

"I switched to focal loss because it's used in detection."

→ Sounds like engineering judgement.

What the script lands instead

Corpus loop: a measured signal said the rare-class F1 was too low on the raw dump.

Schema loop: a measured signal said EVENT_TYPE grounding was at 58%, below the auditability threshold.

Loss loop: the §6.6 ablation measured a +11 F1 lift on VICTIM.

→ Reads as design-science iteration.

The trap. If you say "I felt that..." or "I thought..." anywhere on this slide, the methodological frame collapses. Replace every internal-mental-state verb with an external-signal verb: "the rare-class F1 was low", "the grounding score fell below the threshold", "the ablation measured a lift". Signals are external. Preferences are internal.

5 · The three reserved numbers

The slide presents the loops qualitatively. But you should carry three specific numbers in your pocket — to deploy only if a panellist presses on a loop. Pre-loading them in the script would be over-claiming; withholding them and deploying them on demand is the design-science move.

Loss loop

The deployment rule

  • Default delivery: qualitative. Each loop = what it accomplished, no numbers.
  • If a panellist asks "how do you know that worked?" Deploy the relevant reserved number. One number per question — don't fire all three at once.
  • If a panellist asks "what numbers back up the loop decisions?" Deploy all three in sequence: "Kappa zero point seven eight on schema. Two hundred twelve thousand down to fifty thousand on corpus diversity. Plus eleven F1 on VICTIM from the loss ablation. Each loop closed on a measured signal."
  • Critical: these three numbers also appear on later slides (11, 12, 18) with full context. Deploying them here pre-empts the panel's later interest and signals you have data behind every loop.
Why withhold the numbers on the slide itself? Three reasons. (1) The slide is about methodology, not results — putting numbers here would shift the panel's attention to "is +11 F1 a lot?" instead of "is this design science?". (2) The numbers get full context on dedicated later slides; previewing weakens them. (3) Withholding-then-deploying signals confidence — the candidate has more evidence than the slide shows.

6 · Three pre-loaded interrupts

"Is this just engineering? You built something and tested it."
"Engineering would mean making implementation choices to ship. Design science means iterating on a research artefact with a measured signal closing each loop. The three loops on screen each closed with a specific signal — schema grounding score, rare-class F1 on the raw corpus, the section 6.6 ablation. The iteration was empirically driven; that's what differentiates design science from engineering. The §6.6 ablation in particular is a controlled experiment embedded in the broader design-science programme."
The key sentence: "engineering makes implementation choices to ship; design science iterates on a research artefact with measured signals closing each loop." That contrast is the entire defence.
"Why design science instead of a controlled experiment as your primary methodology?"
"Controlled experiments fit single-technique claims — you isolate one variable, vary it, measure the effect. VioNER's claim is about an integrated artefact — schema plus model plus KB plus platform — evaluated across multiple criteria (F1, KB enrichment, UAT). Design science is the established frame for that kind of multi-criterion integrated-artefact contribution. The §6.6 loss ablation is the embedded controlled experiment within the larger design-science programme; both methodologies coexist."
Concede that controlled experiments are embedded — that's honest and pre-empts the "you're just choosing a softer label" follow-up. Hevner 2004 and Peffers 2007 are the canonical design-science references if cited.
"What if one of the loops had failed — would you have published the failure?"
"Each loop's exit criterion was empirical, not preference-based. If the schema loop hadn't produced higher agreement than the proposal's 26-entity schema, I would have kept iterating until it did, or documented why convergence wasn't possible. The grounding pilot measured EVENT_TYPE at 58% — that signal closed the loop on dropping EVENT_TYPE from the training signal. The exit criterion is the criterion; failure modes are visible in the iteration record."
The point to land: the exit criterion is the criterion. Loops aren't validated by their outcome — they're validated by whether a measured signal closed them. This is a methodologically subtle but important defence.

7 · The hero close

The hero line — memorise verbatim "Each loop was a design decision validated by measurement, not preference. That's what makes this genuine design science."

Why this line is the hero

  • "Measurement, not preference" is a binary contrast — easy to remember, easy to recall under pressure. Binary contrasts land harder than gradients.
  • "Each loop was..." includes every iteration without enumerating. The panel doesn't have to track three separate validations — they hear one categorical claim.
  • "Genuine design science" closes with the methodology name. The slide title and the hero line both anchor on it; the panel hears it twice.
  • Internally consistent with slides 1-8. Slide 4 framed analyst work as operational, not perceptual; slide 7 framed gaps operationally, not technologically; slide 9 frames iteration empirically, not preferentially. The pattern of "external signal over internal feeling" runs through the entire deck.
