1 · Where this slide sits in the arc

Slides 10 and 11 set the data side — the schema, the taxonomy, the corpus. Slide 12 is where the modelling contribution becomes visible. It is the loss function — the part of the system that decides what the model pays attention to. The 78 % O dominance of the training corpus is the problem this slide solves; the two rebalancers are the solution.

The function of slide 12. Two jobs. (1) Show that the choice of loss function was principled — each ingredient has a named role and solves a named sub-problem. (2) Make the complementarity claim memorable enough that when slide 18 shows the ablation (+11 F1 on VICTIM), the panel already believes the story this slide tells. Without complementarity established here, the ablation on slide 18 reads as "we combined two things and it worked" — which sounds lucky. With complementarity established, the ablation reads as "we predicted the combination would win, and the experiment confirmed it" — which sounds principled.

3 · The "two rebalancers, two axes" mental model

The imbalance problem has two axes the training has to attack — and each ingredient targets one of them. Hold onto this distinction; it is the entire complementarity argument.

Axis 1 — across classes

"Some classes are rare"

78 % of tokens are O. Rare entity classes like VICTIM appear in single-digit-% shares of the corpus. Plain training puts equal per-token weight on common and rare — the rare classes get drowned out.

The rebalancer: class weights. Bigger weight on rare classes.

Axis 2 — across difficulty (within a class)

"Some tokens are easy"

Within a class, some tokens are easy — an O between two punctuation marks the model gets within the first epoch. The model spends gradient polishing easy-correct tokens instead of solving hard ones.

The rebalancer: focal loss. (1 − p)² shrinks the easy ones.

Why each ingredient is needed. Class weights alone fix axis 1 — VICTIM tokens count more — but still treat every VICTIM token equally. The model can still spend its capacity polishing easy VICTIMs (e.g., "civilians" right after "killed") while neglecting hard ones. Focal loss alone fixes axis 2 — hard tokens count more — but if VICTIM tokens overall are still rare in count, focal loss alone cannot lift the per-class signal high enough. The two ingredients attack different axes, so neither does the other's job. The combination is not redundant; it is required.

4 · The focal-loss spotlight analogy

Class weights are the easy intuition — bigger weight on rare classes. Focal loss is the one the panel may want to hear in plain English. The image to carry is a teacher with 100 students:

The spotlight. A teacher with 100 students — 95 already understand, 5 are struggling. Uniform attention (= cross-entropy) wastes most of the energy on the 95 who don't need it. Focal loss installs a spotlight that automatically brightens on confused students and dims on confident ones. The γ parameter controls how aggressive the spotlight is. γ = 0 — uniform attention. γ = 2 — sharp spotlight, the value used here. γ = 5 — too sharp, even moderately confident students fade and training destabilises.

6 · The complementarity claim — the core skill

Slide 12's central craft skill is the complementarity claim: when you use two techniques together, the defence requires showing they attack different parts of the problem. Otherwise you are double-counting — paying twice for the same gain. The defence has three compulsory moves.

The three-move complementarity defence

1Name the two ingredients. "Inverse-frequency class weights and focal loss." Use the technical names; the panel knows them.
2Name the two distinct axes they attack. "Across classes" vs "across difficulty." This is the load-bearing move — the answer survives only if the two axes are genuinely different.
3Point to the ablation as evidence. "Slide 18 / §6.6 shows neither alone reaches the combined performance." The ablation table is what converts the claim from asserted to measured.

The two-axes mental picture

Combination = signal lifted on rare AND hard tokens at once
The trap. If asked "why both?", do not say "they both fix imbalance" — that sounds redundant and reads as double-counting. Say "they fix different aspects of imbalance." Distinct axes is the whole defence. Without naming the two axes by name, the answer collapses.

The deployment rule

  • Default delivery: name the two ingredients, name the two axes, cite 130×, cite γ = 2, end on complementarity + ablation reference.
  • If asked "why cap at 10?" Deploy "empirical — without it the rarest class weight hits about twenty-two and destabilises early training. Ten keeps rare classes elevated without exploding the gradients."

8 · Four pre-loaded interrupts

"Why focal loss and class weights — isn't using both double-counting?"
"No — they attack different parts of the imbalance problem. Class weights rebalance across classes: VICTIM tokens become about a hundred and thirty times more loss-impactful than O tokens. Focal loss rebalances within a class: easy-correct tokens get a (one minus p) squared factor that nearly zeroes them out, so the optimiser spends capacity on hard cases. Class weights alone still let the model polish easy VICTIMs; focal alone cannot lift the per-class signal enough when VICTIM is rare to begin with. The ablation in section 6.6, on slide 18, shows neither alone reaches the combined performance."
Two-axes framing is the entire defence. Never say "they both help with imbalance" — say "they fix different aspects of imbalance." The §6.6 / slide-18 reference is what converts the claim from asserted to measured.
"Why γ = 2 and not 1 or 3?"
"Lin et al. 2017's recommended default from the original focal-loss paper. We tested both alternatives. Gamma equal to one gave smaller gains — the spotlight wasn't sharp enough to suppress confident-correct tokens. Gamma equal to three destabilised training in the first few hundred steps because moderately-confident tokens were also fading out, leaving the optimiser only catastrophic cases. Two is the empirically stable choice for our O-dominance and our learning rate."
Four moves: cite the paper, name the alternatives, give the symptom of each, name "empirically stable" as the criterion. The "empirically stable" phrase ties back to slide 9's measurement-not-preference posture.
"Why cap the class weights at 10?"
"Empirical. Without the cap, the rarest classes — VICTIM and CASUALTIES — come out with weights around twenty-two, and the resulting gradients destabilise training in the first few hundred steps. Ten keeps the rare-class weights high enough to lift the signal without producing the gradient blow-up. Finding the right cap was iterative — section 5.5 of the thesis documents the search."
Don't volunteer the math on the unclipped weights — say "around twenty-two" and move on. Don't lead with "the literature suggests" — this was an empirical choice for our corpus, not a literature default. The §5.5 reference is the audit trail.
"Did you try other losses — dice, only label smoothing, contrastive?"
"Label smoothing at beta zero point one is in fact in the production loss already — its contribution is marginal but kept for completeness. Dice loss is a fair future comparison but wasn't tested in this thesis; focal loss has more direct prior work in token classification under severe imbalance, which is why it was the principal candidate. Contrastive losses are out of scope — they fit representation learning, not the classification-head fine-tuning regime here."
Concede label smoothing's presence honestly, defer dice loss to future work without pretending it was tested, and rule out contrastive on architectural grounds (it's a different training regime). The pattern: concede what you did, defer what's fair, refuse what's a category error.

9 · The hero close

The hero line — memorise verbatim "The two attack different parts of the imbalance — class weights across classes, focal loss across difficulty. The combination beats either alone."

Why this line is the hero

  • "Different parts" names and refuses the double-counting attack inside your own sentence — the same pattern as slide 10's "didn't get smaller as a concession" and slide 11's "not a relabelling." The objection is neutralised before it's asked.
  • "Across classes" vs "across difficulty" are the two axes — the parallel structure makes the distinction memorable. Saying both axes in one breath is what plants the mental picture of two orthogonal rebalancers.
  • "Beats either alone" teases the slide-18 ablation without spending its number. The panel hears "this claim has measured evidence behind it" without losing the +11 F1 punchline that lives on slide 18.
  • Consistent with the deck's posture. Slide 9: measurement, not preference. Slide 10: decomposed, not narrower. Slide 11: synthesis, not relabelling. Slide 12: complementary, not redundant. Every contribution slide names the dismissive reading and refuses it with a precise positive claim.

11 · Quiz

Q1. The panel asks: "Why use focal loss and class weights? Isn't that double-counting?"

Pick the strongest response.

  • "Both help with class imbalance, so using both is safer than relying on either."
  • "Class weights are the standard approach, and focal loss adds extra robustness."
  • "No — they attack different parts of the imbalance problem. Class weights rebalance across classes — a VICTIM token becomes about 130× more loss-impactful than an O token. Focal loss rebalances across difficulty within a class — the (1−p)² factor suppresses easy-correct tokens so the optimiser concentrates on hard ones. Class weights alone still let the model polish easy VICTIMs; focal alone cannot lift the per-class signal enough when VICTIM is rare. The ablation on slide 18 shows neither alone reaches the combined performance."
  • "Focal loss covers the cases that class weights miss."

Q2. What is the role of the (1−py)γ term in the focal loss formula?

Pick the cleanest explanation.

  • It normalises the loss across class sizes.
  • It is a per-token modulator that becomes near zero when the model is confident and correct (py high), and approaches 1 when the model is uncertain (py low). Effect: the optimiser spends gradient on hard tokens, not on easy-correct ones.
  • It penalises overconfident predictions during inference.
  • It is a smoothing term that prevents the loss from becoming infinite.

Q3. The panel asks: "Why cap class weights at 10?"

Pick the strongest response.

  • "Ten is the literature default."
  • "To keep the loss values in a reasonable numerical range."
  • "Ten worked best in our validation experiments."
  • "Empirical — without the cap, VICTIM and CASUALTIES come out with weights around 22, which destabilises training in the first few hundred steps with gradient blow-up. Ten keeps the rare-class weights elevated without producing instability. The search is documented in §5.5."

Q4. What does the "130×" number on the slide refer to?

Pick the correct meaning.

  • A VICTIM token contributes about 130× the loss-weight of an O token under the production recipe (class weight ≈ 10 vs class weight ≈ 0.075).
  • The focal loss is 130× more sensitive to hard examples than plain cross-entropy.
  • Training with the recipe is 130× faster to converge than plain cross-entropy.
  • The combined model achieves 130× higher recall on VICTIM than the cross-entropy baseline.
