Lesson 04 · Slide 1 — Opening

Deliver the opening 45 seconds with composure

Slide 1 is the highest-anxiety moment of the entire defence. Voice not yet warm, nerves at peak, the panel forming their first impression. This lesson gives you a delivery you can deliver even shaking.

Mission link. Survive the riskiest 45 seconds. Plant the headline. Skill type. Composure + presentation craft. Time. 15 minutes.

Lesson 3 taught you to frame the talk on slide 2. But slide 2 is the second moment, not the first. Before any of that comes the opening — slide 1, where you walk in, greet the panel, and have to be the most composed you've been all day. This lesson teaches you three things: the script, the headline-plant, and a concrete anxiety-management protocol for the moments before you speak.

1 · What's on the screen

The panel sees this. It's a static title slide — they're not looking at the text after the first three seconds. They're looking at you.

Slide 1 of 22
VioNER
Violent-event Named Entity Recognition for African News
Binalfew Kassa

Master's Thesis Defence · AAU Computer Science Department · 2026

Where the panel's attention is. For the first 3 seconds they read the slide. From second 4 onward they are watching you — your posture, your eye contact, your voice. The slide content matters less than how you appear during the opening 45 seconds. This is a delivery moment, not a content moment.

2 · Before you speak — the first-5-seconds protocol

You will be nervous. That is normal. Nervous can still look composed if you do exactly this in the first five seconds after taking your position:

The first-5-seconds protocol

  1. Anchor your feet. Stand still. Shoulder-width. Don't pace, don't shift weight. This single move signals composure to the panel before you say anything.
  2. One anchor breath. Slow inhale through the nose for three seconds. Slow exhale through the mouth for three seconds. The panel reads this as a confident pause. You use it to slow your heart rate.
  3. Eye contact with the chair. Find the most senior panel member. One second of direct eye contact. This says "I'm ready."
  4. Now speak. Start with the greeting (Beat 1 below). Not before. Don't rush past these four steps because they feel slow — they feel slow to you but the panel reads them as composure.

This protocol is the difference between a defender who looks scared and one who looks ready. Practice it before each rehearsal so it becomes automatic.

3 · The 45-second script, in three beats

Three short paragraphs. Each ~15 seconds. The structure: greet and name the work → roadmap and plant 0.909 → state the three-part frame.

1
Beat 1 — greet and name the work ~15 seconds
"Good morning, distinguished examiners. I'm Binalfew Kassa. The thesis I'm presenting today is VioNER — Violent-event Named Entity Recognition. It's an end-to-end, deployable system that converts English-language news about violent events in Africa into structured, queryable 5W1H records."
The greeting is non-negotiable. "Good morning" or "good afternoon" depending on slot. Land your name slowly — first impression beats everything else here.
2
Beat 2 — roadmap and plant the headline ~15 seconds
"In the next thirty minutes I'll cover the problem, the four-part contribution, the evidence, and the limits I own honestly. The headline result I want to plant early: micro F1 of 0.909 on held-out data, with rare-entity recovery that actually saves analyst time."
"Plant early" is the strategic verb. It signals: this is the number to remember. Everything later in the talk will land against this reference point.
3
Beat 3 — the three-part frame ~15 seconds
"The frame for the whole talk: a real operational bottleneck, methodologically open in the literature, closed by an integrated artefact I built and evaluated end-to-end."
End the sentence on "end-to-end". Pause briefly. Then advance to slide 2 and deliver the roadmap (lesson 3). The transition from slide 1 to slide 2 is seamless — Beat 3 here primes the integration thread there.

4 · The 0.909 plant — what it's doing

Stating the headline number on slide 1 looks innocuous. It is doing strategic work.

The number to plant
0.909
micro F1 on held-out 10,000-example validation set · slide 16's headline arrives prepared
Three reasons to plant 0.909 in slide 1, not save it for slide 16.
  • Anchor effect. Once stated, every later number in the talk gets compared to it. The +11 F1 ablation lift, the per-entity F1s, the macro F1 — all land against this anchor.
  • Composure signal. Stating your best number early says "I'm not hiding anything; this work earned its result." Holding it for slide 16 looks like you're saving it for impact, which the panel reads as theatrical.
  • Pre-empts the "is this any good?" question. By minute 1 the panel has a benchmark for what your model achieves. Every subsequent claim is calibrated against that benchmark.

Notice the script doesn't say "my best result is 0.909" or "I want to highlight 0.909". It says "the headline result I want to plant early." The word "plant" tells the panel this is intentional, not boastful.

5 · The three-part frame

The last sentence of slide 1 is a compressed argument shape. Memorise these three parts in order — they're the spine of the entire talk.

1
Real operational bottleneckAnalysts at AU-CEWS, ACLED, UNHCR doing 15-25 min/article. About 30,000 events/year. The problem is real.
2
Methodologically open in the literatureThe four operational gaps haven't been closed by any existing system. Slides 6-7 demonstrate this.
3
Closed by an integrated artefact, evaluated end-to-endThe five contributions, integrated and tested with UAT. Slides 10-19 present them.

If you remember nothing else from slide 1, remember those three steps. They are what the rest of the talk delivers, and stating them upfront tells the panel "this is the argument I'm making".

6 · Body language for the opening

Four concrete techniques. Each is small. Together they decide whether you look ready or rattled.

Hands

At your sides, or one resting on the podium. Move them naturally as you speak.

Not in pockets. Not crossed. Not gripping the slide remote like a stress ball.

Eyes

Rotate across the panel. Settle on one face for 5-7 seconds, then move to the next.

Not on your notes. Not on the slide. Not on the floor.

Voice

Project to the back wall of the room. Slightly louder than feels natural.

Not into the podium microphone if there is no mic. Not at conversation volume.

Pace

Slower than normal speech. 130-150 words per minute. Pause briefly between beats.

Not racing through to get it over with. Not pausing so long the panel wonders if you froze.

7 · Timed practice

The whole 45-second script, end to end. Including the first-5-seconds protocol — start the timer when you start the anchor breath, stop when you finish "end-to-end" on Beat 3. Target: 50-65 seconds (the protocol adds ~5 sec to the 45 sec script).

0.0 s
The hardest part of slide 1. The first 10 seconds, while you're still warming up. If you can deliver Beat 1 without a tremor in your voice, the rest of the talk is downhill. Practice Beat 1 cold — that is, walking into the room from another activity and delivering it immediately. That simulates the actual moment.

8 · Quiz

Q1. Why is 0.909 stated on slide 1, not saved for slide 16?

Pick the strongest reason.

  • Because the panel might miss the number if it's only stated once on slide 16.
  • Because slide 16 is too late in the talk — the panel won't be paying attention.
  • It anchors every later number to this benchmark, signals composure (no number-hiding), and pre-empts the "is this any good?" question by minute 1.
  • Because the headline number is the most important thing to mention, and slide 1 is the most-watched slide.

Q2. You take your position at the front of the room. What do you do in the next 5 seconds, BEFORE speaking?

Pick the right protocol.

  • Immediately begin the greeting. Any silence makes you look unprepared.
  • Anchor your feet, take one slow breath, make eye contact with the chair, then begin. The pause reads as composure, not hesitation.
  • Glance at your notes one final time, then start.
  • Smile at every panel member in turn before you start. It establishes warmth.

Q3. The three-part frame on Beat 3 is:

Recall the exact three steps in order.

  • Real operational bottleneck → methodologically open in the literature → closed by an integrated artefact, evaluated end-to-end.
  • Theoretical problem → academic gap → engineering solution.
  • Research question → methodology → results.
  • Problem → contribution → evidence.

9 · Fluency checklist

Tick each mentally. The opening is one-shot — you only get one chance per defence. Drill until all seven are ticked.

  • I can execute the first-5-seconds protocol (anchor feet, one breath, eye contact with chair, then speak) without rushing.
  • I can deliver Beat 1 with no voice tremor, even cold.
  • I can state "micro F1 of 0.909 on held-out data" without stumbling on the number.
  • I can recite the three-part frame in order: bottleneck → open in literature → integrated artefact.
  • I end Beat 3 on "end-to-end" with a clean stop — no trailing "so" or "um".
  • I make eye contact with at least two panel members during the 45 seconds.
  • I can explain in one sentence WHY 0.909 is planted in slide 1 and not saved for slide 16.
If all seven are ticked, your opening 45 seconds — the riskiest moment of the entire defence — is armoured. From slide 2 onward, you have momentum. The panel has heard you sound composed and seen you state a substantial headline number. The rest of the talk lands inside that frame.

Where to go next

When you're ready, ask:

  • "Teach me what to say on slide 22 — the close." (Restating the five contributions, the final ask, calm landing.)
  • "Drill me cold on slide 1 — I'll deliver it from memory and you score it."
  • "Teach me how to handle a panellist who interrupts during slide 1." (Rare but high-stakes.)
  • "Teach me the slide-to-slide transitions" (1 → 2, 2 → 3, etc — the small gaps between slides).
